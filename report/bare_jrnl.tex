\begin{abstract}
Interacting Maps infers a scene interpretation from an event camera not by a processing pipeline but by a set of retinotopic maps --- intensity, spatial gradient, optical flow and angular velocity --- coupled by weak geometric constraints and refined until mutually consistent. Two formulations exist, by Cook \emph{et al.}~\cite{cook2011,martel2015relational} and in Martel's dissertation~\cite{martel2019thesis}, and neither, to our knowledge, has a public implementation. We implement both on a shared camera model and evaluate them on five rotation sequences, three real and two synthetic. As published, neither formulation tracks the angular velocity, whose scale the constraints leave undetermined: on the real sequences the estimate stays close to the value it starts with, leaving direction errors of $76$--$85^\circ$, and the two update schemes fail in the same way. The method works only when an absolute angular-velocity reference enters the cost minimised at every iteration; supplying it once at initialisation is not sufficient. With a gyroscope as that reference, the fused estimate matches the gyroscope's own accuracy on the real sequences. We show that the reference need not be a second sensor: a contrast-maximization estimate computed from the same events comes within $1.4$--$2.5^\circ$/s of the gyroscope anchor on the three real sequences, and the network improves on that estimate by $13$--$20\%$. The residual error is almost entirely a magnitude error, governed by the ratio of the two step sizes that act on the angular velocity, and lens distortion belongs in the camera model rather than in the events. For the reconstructed intensity the order reverses: at the configuration chosen for the angular velocity, the network without an anchor gives the best image, and the two outputs call for different accumulation windows.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{E}{vent} cameras are bio-inspired vision sensors whose pixels operate independently and asynchronously: instead of sampling a full frame at a fixed rate, every pixel emits an \emph{event} as soon as the logarithmic brightness it observes has changed by more than a fixed contrast threshold~\cite{lichtsteiner2008}. The resulting output is a sparse stream of timestamped, signed brightness changes rather than a sequence of images. This buys microsecond temporal resolution, a high dynamic range of about $140$~dB, low power consumption and little motion blur, which makes such sensors attractive for high-speed and high-dynamic-range robotics~\cite{gallego2022survey}. It also means that essentially none of the frame-based computer-vision toolbox transfers directly: because a single event carries almost no information on its own, algorithms have to be redesigned around the data they actually receive.

Most event-based methods answer this by keeping the familiar \emph{processor-centric} structure of a pipeline: events are aggregated into some intermediate representation, which is then transformed step by step into the quantity of interest. A markedly different answer was given by Cook \emph{et al.} with \emph{Interacting Maps}~\cite{cook2011}, later formalised as a relational network~\cite{martel2015relational}. There, the visual interpretation is not computed by a chain of processing stages but is \emph{represented} by several retinotopic maps (light intensity $\Imap$, its spatial gradient $\Gmap$ and the optical flow $\Fmap$) and the camera's angular velocity $\wvec$, for a purely rotating camera, all recurrently coupled by a small number of constraints taken from geometry and from the physics of image formation. The events are the only input and every other quantity is inferred; in~\cite{cook2011} they enter directly as the temporal intensity derivative map $\Vmap$. The maps are updated iteratively, each update reducing the disagreement between the maps a constraint links, so the estimate is obtained as the fixed point of this process rather than from a single forward pass. 

Martel later generalised the idea in his dissertation~\cite{martel2019thesis} into a \emph{quantity-centric} framework: a bipartite graph whose nodes are visual quantities and whose factors are explicit energy functions, with inference performed by message passing. Cook's model, which covers only a purely rotating camera, becomes one case of this framework, and Martel derives further cases that add temporal persistence, 6-DoF motion with depth, a binocular setup, or fusion with an inertial measurement unit (IMU).

Each constraint links two or more unknown maps, so from $\Vmap$ alone none of $\Imap$, $\Gmap$, $\Fmap$, $\wvec$ can be computed on its own; a solution is determined only when all constraints are enforced together, and even then only up to a global scale (Secs.~\ref{sec:model} and~\ref{sec:beta}). Later event-based methods also estimate several quantities jointly, although they start from different premises: Kim \emph{et al.}~\cite{kim2014} track the camera rotation while building an intensity mosaic of the scene, and the contrast-maximization framework of Gallego \emph{et al.}~\cite{gallego2018cmax} searches for the motion that makes the motion-compensated events sharpest.

Despite being regularly cited as an early example of joint inference from events, neither formulation appears to have a publicly available implementation, and to our knowledge no independent evaluation of either has been published. Both descriptions also leave out details that turn out to matter a great deal in practice: the relaxation step sizes, how the camera is calibrated, including for lens distortion, how the maps are initialised beyond ``random data'', and, in Cook's sequential scheme, the order in which the maps are updated.

Most importantly, both note the scale ambiguity shared by all inferred maps, but treat the absolute reference only briefly: Cook resolves the ambiguity arbitrarily in the first frame, and Martel lists ways to fix it (a gyroscope, absolute intensities, or a persistent intensity map with an additional flow estimate) but shows only qualitative results and does not say, for the gyroscope, whether the clamp holds through every iteration or how the readings are synchronised with the event frames. Reproducing the method therefore means making these choices explicitly and reporting what they cost. 

This report describes such a re-implementation and its evaluation. Our contributions are:
    \begin{itemize}
\item An implementation of both formulations, Cook's sequential relaxation and Martel's two-phase message passing, on one shared data path and camera model, extended to include lens distortion, evaluated on the rotation sequences of the Event-Camera Dataset~\cite{mueggler2017} and on synthetic ECRot sequences~\cite{guo2024cmaxslam}, including a sensitivity analysis of the main parameters.
\item An empirical account of the scale ambiguity that Cook~\cite{cook2011} and Martel~\cite{martel2019thesis} mention but neither quantifies (Sec.~\ref{sec:beta_exp}): without an absolute reference the direction of $\wvec$ becomes uninformative, and the reference must enter at every iteration, not only at initialisation.
\item A sensor-free reference for $\wvec$: contrast maximization~\cite{gallego2018cmax}, whose objective is not scale-invariant, replaces the gyroscope in two ways: as the target of the anchor cost, solved once per frame, or as the update rule for $\wvec$, one contrast gradient step per iteration.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the report is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background} introduces the model and its two variants. Section~\ref{sec:implementation} describes how we implement the two variants, how we address the scale ambiguity, how the system was developed, and how we evaluate it. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports the results on the angular velocity, Section~\ref{sec:recon_experiments} those on the reconstructed intensity, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\subsection{The Common Model: Quantities and Constraints}
\label{sec:model}
Both formulations describe a camera rotating in a static scene with the same quantities, tied together by three constraints. This subsection introduces the input, the quantities and the constraints, and shows why no constraint can be inverted on its own.

\subsubsection{Input}
An event camera pixel fires an event $e_k=(x_k,y_k,t_k,p_k)$ as soon as the logarithmic intensity it observes has changed by a contrast threshold $\theta$ since the last event at that pixel~\cite{lichtsteiner2008}:
\begin{equation}
p_k = \begin{cases}
+1 & \text{if } \Delta \log \Imap(x_k,y_k,t_k) > \theta,\\
-1 & \text{if } \Delta \log \Imap(x_k,y_k,t_k) < -\theta .
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Events are accumulated over a short window of duration $\Delta t$ into an \emph{event frame}, the per-pixel signed event count
\begin{equation}
E(x,y) \;=\; \sum_{t_k \in [t, t+\Delta t)} p_k \, \delta(x-x_k)\,\delta(y-y_k),
\label{eq:eventframe}
\end{equation}
which is a direct correlate of the temporal derivative of the (log) intensity.
Cook \emph{et al.}~\cite{cook2011} use a scalar map $\Vmap \approx \partial \Imap/\partial t$ derived from~\eqref{eq:eventframe} as the \emph{only} input to the network;
Martel~\cite{martel2015relational,martel2019thesis} keeps $E$ as the observation and infers $\Vmap$ from it. In what follows $\Imap$ always denotes log-intensity, as is standard for event cameras.

\subsubsection{Quantities}
The world is modelled as a static scene observed by a camera that is fixed in position but free to rotate. Besides $\Vmap$, the model carries the log-intensity map $\Imap$, its spatial gradient $\Gmap$, the optical flow $\Fmap$, and the camera's angular velocity $\wvec\in\mathbb{R}^3$, the only non-retinotopic quantity in the network. A constant map $\Cmat$ encodes the camera geometry.

\subsubsection{Constraints}
Three relations tie these quantities together. The first is the optical-flow constraint equation, which follows from the brightness-constancy assumption~\cite{horn1981}. It states that the brightness of each point of the image pattern stays constant as it moves, so its total derivative vanishes
$\mathrm{d}\Imap/\mathrm{d}t = 0$:
\begin{equation}
\Vmap_{x,y} + \Fmap_{x,y}\cdot\Gmap_{x,y} \;=\; 0 .
\label{eq:ofce}
\end{equation}
The second is the definition of the gradient, discretised with forward differences,
\begin{equation}
\Gmap_{x,y} \;=\; \nabla \Imap_{x,y}
 \;=\; \big(\Imap_{x+1,y}-\Imap_{x,y},\; \Imap_{x,y+1}-\Imap_{x,y}\big)^{\!\top}.
\label{eq:spatial}
\end{equation}
The third states that the flow induced by a pure rotation is, at every pixel, a
linear function of $\wvec$,
\begin{equation}
\Fmap_{x,y} \;=\; \Cmat_{x,y}\,\wvec ,
\label{eq:kin}
\end{equation}
with the $2\times3$ kinematic matrix obtained by differentiating the projection of a rotating 3-D point~\cite{martel2019thesis}
\begin{equation}
\Cmat_{x,y} =
\begin{pmatrix}
 x'y' & -(x'^2+1) & y' \\[2pt]
 y'^2+1 & -x'y' & -x'
\end{pmatrix},
\label{eq:cmat}
\end{equation}
where $(x',y')$ are the normalised image coordinates of pixel $(x,y)$; the flow in pixels is this matrix premultiplied by $\operatorname{diag}(f_x,f_y)$, as in~\eqref{eq:cdist}. Notably $\Cmat_{x,y}$ does not depend on scene depth: rotational flow carries no structure information, which is what makes the purely rotational model self-contained.

\subsubsection{Underdetermination of the individual constraints}
Each constraint on its own is too weak to invert. Equation~\eqref{eq:ofce} is one scalar equation in the four unknowns $(\Fmap_{x,y},\Gmap_{x,y})$: given $\Vmap$ and $\Gmap$, the admissible $\Fmap$ form a line perpendicular to $\Gmap$ causing the aperture problem.
Equation~\eqref{eq:spatial} does not constrain $\Imap$ at all in the direction of a constant offset, and $\Cmat_{x,y}$ in~\eqref{eq:kin} has rank~2 with kernel spanned by $(x',y',1)^{\!\top}$~\cite{martel2019thesis}, so a single flow vector leaves a whole line of admissible $\wvec$. Only when all three are imposed simultaneously, over all pixels, does a solution become determined (up to the scale ambiguity below). There is no feed-forward order in which $\Imap$, $\Gmap$, $\Fmap$ and $\wvec$ can be computed from $\Vmap$; the interpretation has to be found by iterating the constraints against each other.

\subsection{Scale Ambiguity}
\label{sec:beta}
One degeneracy survives even the joint problem. For any $\beta\neq 0$ the substitution
\begin{equation}
(\Imap,\Gmap,\Fmap,\wvec) \;\longmapsto\;
 (\beta\Imap,\;\beta\Gmap,\;\Fmap/\beta,\;\wvec/\beta)
\label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}
leaves \eqref{eq:ofce}--\eqref{eq:kin} satisfied identically. From a single input frame it is therefore impossible to tell a slowly moving high-contrast scene from a quickly moving low-contrast one, and since $\beta$ may be negative, not even the sign of $\wvec$ is determined. 

Cook \emph{et al.} note that in practice the ambiguity is resolved arbitrarily on the first frame by the random initialisation and then held roughly constant by warm-starting each frame from the previous converged state~\cite{cook2011}. Martel describes the same ambiguity in his formulation~\cite{martel2019thesis}: without a reference, small variations such as the initialisation decide the sign of $\beta$, and the network can switch to the opposite solution during abrupt changes of motion, which appears as inverted intensities (``colour flips''). 

Since $\beta$ couples all inferred maps, fixing any one of them in absolute units removes it. He gives three ways of doing so: clamping $\wvec$ to gyroscope readings, feeding absolute intensities from an ATIS sensor into $\Imap$, or keeping the previous intensity frame together with a block-matching optical flow and the frame duration, so that $\Fmap$ is expressed in pixels per unit time. To clamp a quantity, he writes the sensor reading into it whenever one arrives and removes it from the quantities the network updates; with $\wvec$ clamped to the gyroscope, the network scales all other maps through it, which removes the colour flips. All three are demonstrated qualitatively only. In our implementation we build on the first of these remedies, for the Martel network only (Sec.~\ref{sec:scale}), while the Cook network keeps the mechanism of its source. 

\subsection{Variant I: Sequential Relaxation (Cook \emph{et al.})}
\label{sec:cook}

Cook \emph{et al.}~\cite{cook2011,martel2015relational} derive the updates from a single recipe: to
update a map from one relation, assume the other maps in that relation are
correct, compute the \emph{candidate} map --- the closest map to the current one
that would satisfy the relation --- and take a small step of size $\delta$
towards it. \st{This is a relaxation method applied to local constraints rather than
to a boundary-constrained differential equation.}

For the nonlinear relation~\eqref{eq:ofce} the candidate is not unique, so the
constraint is turned into the quadratic error
$Q_{\Vmap,\Gmap}(\Fmap_{x,y}) = (\Vmap_{x,y}+\Fmap_{x,y}\cdot\Gmap_{x,y})^2$ and
a gradient step is taken,
\begin{equation}
\Fmap_{x,y} \leftarrow \Fmap_{x,y}
 - 2\,\delta_{V\!F\!G}\,
   (\Vmap_{x,y}+\Fmap_{x,y}\cdot\Gmap_{x,y})\,\Gmap_{x,y},
\label{eq:cook_ofce}
\end{equation}
and symmetrically for $\Gmap$. For the linear relations the candidate is
explicit. From~\eqref{eq:spatial},
$\Gmap_{x,y}\leftarrow(1-\delta_{I\!G})\Gmap_{x,y}+\delta_{I\!G}\nabla
\Imap_{x,y}$; in the opposite direction a change of $\Imap_{x,y}$ also affects
the gradients at the neighbours $(x\!-\!1,y)$ and $(x,y\!-\!1)$, so the residual
$\Psi=\Gmap-\nabla\Imap$ is differenced back,
$\hat\Psi^{(x)}_{x,y}=\Psi^{(x)}_{x,y}-\Psi^{(x)}_{x-1,y}$ (and analogously in
$y$), giving
\begin{equation}
\Imap_{x,y} \leftarrow \Imap_{x,y}
  - \delta_{G\!I}\big(\hat\Psi^{(x)}_{x,y}+\hat\Psi^{(y)}_{x,y}\big),
\label{eq:cook_poisson}
\end{equation}
a discrete Poisson step. 

Finally $\Fmap$ is blended towards the rotational flow, which Cook writes as $m_{32}(\wvec\times\vec c_{x,y})$ with $\vec c_{x,y}$ the unit viewing direction of pixel $(x,y)$ and $m_{32}$ the projection to the image; this is~\eqref{eq:kin} in his notation. In turn, each flow vector leaves a whole line of admissible $\wvec$, and $\wvec$ is blended towards the point with the smallest summed squared distance to all these lines, a linear least-squares fit that aggregates every pixel's weak opinion into one estimate. Cook stores
$\Imap$ on a $(H\!+\!1)\times(W\!+\!1)$ grid so that forward differences yield a
full $H\times W$ gradient.

The updates are applied \textbf{sequentially}: each map is written immediately,
so later updates within the same sweep already see the new values. This is a
Gauss--Seidel scheme, and the result consequently depends on the order in which the relations are visited~\cite{martel2015relational}, an order neither paper
fixes (ours is given in
Sec.~\ref{sec:networks}).

\subsection{Variant II: Energy-Based Message Passing (Martel)}
\label{sec:martel}

Martel~\cite{martel2019thesis} recasts the same system in a
\emph{quantity-centric} framework. A model is a bipartite graph whose nodes are
visual quantities and whose factors are explicit energy functions, in the spirit
of a factor graph~\cite{kschischang2001}. For the rotational model the total energy is
the sum of the four costs
\begin{align}
\mathcal{C}_{\text{tot}} = \sum_{x,y} \Big[ &
  \|\nabla \Imap_{x,y}-\Gmap_{x,y}\|^2
+ (\Vmap_{x,y}+\Fmap_{x,y}\cdot\Gmap_{x,y})^2 \nonumber\\
&+ (\sigma(\Vmap_{x,y})-E_{x,y})^2
+ \|\Fmap_{x,y}-\Cmat_{x,y}\wvec\|^2 \Big],
\label{eq:energy}
\end{align}
where the third term makes the event map $E$ an explicit quantity of the model,
related to $\Vmap$ through the sensor's level-crossing nonlinearity $\sigma$
rather than being identified with it.

Inference minimises~\eqref{eq:energy} greedily by message passing
(Algorithm~6.5 of~\cite{martel2019thesis}) in two phases per iteration. In
\emph{Phase~1} every quantity sends its current value to the costs it
participates in, and each cost computes its partial derivative with respect to
each of its quantities. In \emph{Phase~2} the costs send those derivatives back
and every quantity updates. Because differentiation is linear, a quantity
touched by several costs simply sums the messages it receives; e.g.\ $\Gmap$
receives contributions from both the spatial and the flow-constraint cost.
Nonlinear relations use gradient steps as in~\eqref{eq:cook_ofce}, whereas linear
relations admit a closed-form optimum and are updated by blending towards it. For
the angular velocity, setting $\partial \mathcal{C}/\partial\wvec = 0$ yields the
normal equations $M\wvec^\star = v$ with
\begin{equation}
M=\sum_{x,y}\Cmat_{x,y}^{\!\top}\Cmat_{x,y}, \qquad
v=\sum_{x,y}\Cmat_{x,y}^{\!\top}\Fmap_{x,y},
\label{eq:normaleq}
\end{equation}
and the update $\wvec \leftarrow \wvec - \eta(\wvec - M^{-1}v)$. Since $M$
depends only on the pixel grid and the intrinsics, $M^{-1}$ is a $3\times3$
matrix that can be precomputed once.

The relation $\Gmap=\nabla\Imap$ is linear in the same sense, and the thesis gives two updates for it. Recovering $\Imap$ from $\Gmap$ means solving $\min_{\Imap}\lVert\nabla\Imap-\Gmap\rVert^2$, whose normal equations are the Poisson equation $\Delta\Imap = \operatorname{div}\Gmap$. The first (Eq.~6.61 of~\cite{martel2019thesis}) is an iterative step towards a solution of this equation, with an extra term $-\eta\,\Imap$ that pulls $\Imap$ towards zero at every step, a shrinkage term. The second is the closed-form optimum: because the Laplacian is a convolution it is diagonal in the frequency domain, so the minimiser is one division per spatial frequency $k$,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\Imap}(k) \;=\;
  \frac{\overline{D_x(k)}\,\widehat{\Gmap}_x(k)
      + \overline{D_y(k)}\,\widehat{\Gmap}_y(k)}
       {\lvert D_x(k)\rvert^2+\lvert D_y(k)\rvert^2},
\label{eq:poisson_exact}
\end{equation}
with $D$ the transform of the difference operator. The denominator vanishes at
$k=0$: the mean of $\Imap$ is the constant of integration and is not determined
by $\Gmap$. 

Equation~\eqref{eq:cook_poisson} is one gradient step on the same objective, and~\eqref{eq:poisson_exact} jumps to its minimum; the two differ only in that the step of~\eqref{eq:cook_poisson} converges at a rate ($1-\delta_{G\!I}\big(\lvert D_x(k)\rvert^2+\lvert D_y(k)\rvert^2\big)\approx 1-\delta_{G\!I}\lvert k\rvert^2$) per frequency, so the low frequencies move far more slowly than the high ones. Sec.~\ref{sec:reconstruction} shows this is what separates a recognisable intensity image from an edge map.

The decisive difference to Cook is that Phase~2 happens \textbf{simultaneously}:
all messages are computed from one common snapshot before anything is written.
This is a Jacobi scheme. It removes the dependence on update order and is the
property that makes the algorithm parallelisable and portable to cellular-processor hardware, which is the thesis' motivation for it. The price is propagation speed: since every message is read from the common snapshot, information moves only one relation per iteration. The thesis also derives extensions of the same model: a persistent world map, 6-DoF motion with depth, a binocular variant, and fusion with an inertial measurement unit, in which $\wvec$ is clamped to the gyroscope reading.

\section{Method and Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}
We reproduce both networks of Sec.~\ref{sec:background} in Python with NumPy, on a shared input and camera model. The following subsections describe the two networks, the choices we made where the sources are incomplete or where we depart from them, how we address the scale ambiguity, how the system was developed and validated, and how we
evaluate it.

\subsection{Input and Camera Model}
\label{sec:input}
\label{sec:distortion}

Events are read from the text format of~\cite{mueggler2017} and accumulated per window into the signed-count image~\eqref{eq:eventframe}, the event-frame representation common in the literature~\cite{gallego2022survey}. We then clip it to $\pm c$ and divide by $c$ ($c = 10$), giving $\Vmap \in [-1,1]$. The accumulation window $\Delta t$ serves simultaneously as the input window and as the time step relating the network's internal $R$ (rad/frame) to the reported $\wvec = R/\Delta t$ (rad/s).

Lens distortion can be removed at either end of this path, and both routes are implemented. The first rectifies the event coordinates before accumulation and leaves the kinematic matrix in its ideal pinhole form~\eqref{eq:cmat}. The second keeps the events in their native pixel coordinates and moves the lens into the geometry instead: $\Cmat_{x,y}$ is built from the true undistorted normalised coordinates of each physical pixel and pre-multiplied by the analytic Jacobian $J_D$ of the distortion map, so that the predicted flow is expressed in distorted-pixel units,
\begin{equation}
\Cmat_{x,y} \;=\; \operatorname{diag}(f_x,f_y)\; J_D(x',y')\; A(x',y'),
\label{eq:cdist}
\end{equation}
with $A$ the matrix of~\eqref{eq:cmat}.

The real sequences were recorded with a DAVIS240C~\cite{mueggler2017} whose lens is appreciably barrel-distorted ($k_1 = -0.37$ in the dataset's calibration), and the two routes sample the events differently. Undistortion returns sub-pixel coordinates, which the accumulation~\eqref{eq:eventframe} must bin onto the integer pixel grid. Rectification stretches the periphery, so neighbouring sensor pixels land up to $1.5$\,px apart and binning skips the pixels in between; since the mapping is fixed, the same pixels stay empty in every frame, leaving a concentric moir\'e that is not in the data (Fig.~\ref{fig:distortion}). The $43.2$k pixels of the sensor land on only $33.5$k pixels of the frame, so $\Vmap$ loses about a fifth of its support, and the artefact sits at the scale of one pixel, the scale at which $\Gmap$ differences $\Imap$. 

The second route keeps the events on the pixels that produced them and applies the distortion to the prediction instead, so both networks use it in every result reported below. The contrast-maximization anchor of Sec.~\ref{sec:coupling}, which never bins its coordinates, uses the first route. Sec.~\ref{sec:validation} verifies that the two routes describe the same lens.

\subsection{The Two Networks}
\label{sec:networks}

Both networks implement the update rules of Secs.~\ref{sec:cook} and~\ref{sec:martel}, with the changes listed below. The Cook network applies its updates in place, in the fixed order $\wvec$ from $\Fmap$; $\Fmap$ and then $\Gmap$ from the flow constraint~\eqref{eq:ofce}; $\Gmap$ from $\Imap$; $\Imap$ from $\Gmap$; and $\Fmap$ from $\Cmat\wvec$, and stores $\Imap$ on the $(H\!+\!1)\times(W\!+\!1)$ grid of~\cite{cook2011}. We did not vary this order, so its effect on our results is not measured. The Martel network follows the two-phase scheme of Algorithm~6.5 of~\cite{martel2019thesis} directly: each cost reads the current values of its quantities and adds its partial derivatives to their updates, and the solver only alternates the two phases. A new relation is therefore one new cost, which is how the anchoring and contrast-maximization costs of Sec.~\ref{sec:scale} were added; the solver only had to pass them their external inputs, the anchor target and the events.


\subsection{Deviations From the Sources}
\label{sec:deviations}
None of the sources is complete enough to be implemented without choices. Some of ours fill details the sources leave open, the others depart from them. We list all of them here, since some affect the comparison.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Shared camera model.} Cook expresses the flow relation as $\Fmap = m_{32}(\wvec\times\vec c_{x,y})$ with $\vec c_{x,y}$ the unit  viewing direction of pixel $(x,y)$. We use the kinematic  matrix~\eqref{eq:cmat}, in its distortion-aware form~\eqref{eq:cdist}, and  the same input frames for \emph{both} networks. The two express the same  geometry, and sharing it removes the camera model as a source of difference  between the networks.

\item \textbf{Angular velocity from flow.} Cook minimises the point-to-line distance between $\wvec$ and the family of rotations compatible with each flow vector (Sec.~\ref{sec:cook}); we use the normal equations~\eqref{eq:normaleq} in both networks.

\item \textbf{The event cost is omitted.} The term $(\sigma(\Vmap)-E)^2$ of~\eqref{eq:energy} is not implemented: $\Vmap$ is identified with the normalised event frame rather than inferred from $E$ through the sensor nonlinearity. $\Vmap$ is therefore treated as observed, as in~\cite{cook2011}.

\item \textbf{Intensity update.} Inside the message passing both networks use $\Imap \leftarrow \Imap - \eta\operatorname{div}(\Gmap-\nabla \Imap)$, i.e.\ Cook's~\eqref{eq:cook_poisson}. For Cook's network this is exact: their Eq.~9 is a convex blend whose two $\Imap$ terms cancel. For the Martel network we omit the shrinkage term of the thesis' iterative update (Sec.~\ref{sec:martel});
  %The thesis' Eq.~6.61 carries an additional leading $\Imap$, a shrinkage term, which we therefore omit for the Martel network; 
  Sec.~\ref{sec:regularisers} restores it and measures what it changes. The thesis' exact solution~\eqref{eq:poisson_exact} is also implemented inside the loop, but we use it only as a read-out (item~5); Sec.~\ref{sec:reconstruction} explains why.

\item \textbf{Read-out of $\Imap$.} To read $\Imap$ out of $\Gmap$ we use the thesis' closed-form solution~\eqref{eq:poisson_exact}, computed with the fast Fourier transform (FFT). The FFT treats the image as periodic, so each border continues into the opposite one, whereas the forward differences of the Martel network give zero gradient across the border. We therefore also implemented the same solve with the discrete cosine transform (DCT)~\cite{simchony1990}, which mirrors the image at its borders instead and so matches that convention; none of the sources uses it. Sec.~\ref{sec:reconstruction} compares the two, and we report the FFT read-out. Both return $\Imap$ with its undetermined mean set to zero.
  
%\item \textbf{Read-out of $\Imap$.} Two exact solvers are implemented for reading $\Imap$ out of $\Gmap$. The first is the thesis' solution~\eqref{eq:poisson_exact}, computed with the fast Fourier transform (FFT), which implies periodic boundary conditions. The second, which none of the sources uses, solves the same least-squares problem with the discrete cosine transform (DCT)~\cite{simchony1990}, which implies homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions, i.e.\ zero gradient across the image border. These match the forward differences of the Martel network, whose gradient is zero on the last row and column. Both return $\Imap$ with its undetermined mean set to zero.

\item \textbf{Soft gyroscope clamp.} To solve the $\beta$-ambiguity Martel clamps $\wvec$ to the gyroscope reading; we replace the clamp by a soft anchoring cost (Sec.~\ref{sec:scale}).
  
\item \textbf{Initialisation.} Instead of the random initialisation of~\cite{cook2011,martel2015relational}, both networks start from a \emph{mutually consistent} state: the gyroscope's $\wvec$ and $\Fmap = \Cmat\wvec$~\eqref{eq:kin}. If $\wvec$ is initialised from a known value but $\Fmap$ is left at zero, the kinematics cost sees a large residual and drives $\wvec$ to zero within a few iterations, before the flow constraint has built any structure; we observed this with Martel's simultaneous scheme.
  
\item \textbf{Clipping.} None of the sources clips; Cook \emph{et al.} state that their network needs no clipping of outliers~\cite{cook2011}. Both our networks clip $\Imap$, $\Gmap$ and $\Fmap$ to fixed ranges after every iteration. The Martel network also clips $\wvec$, and clips the flow-constraint gradient of~\eqref{eq:cook_ofce} per pixel, since it grows cubically when $|\Fmap|$ and $|\Gmap|$ are both large.

\item \textbf{Step sizes.} None of the sources reports the values of the relaxation step sizes. Two of ours, $\delta_{F\!R}$ and the anchor weight $\delta_{\text{anchor}}$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:scale}, were set after a parameter sweep and the other four by hand (Table~\ref{tab:params}); Sec.~\ref{sec:sensitivity} examines all six.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Addressing the Scale Ambiguity}
\label{sec:scale}

Neither network removes the $\beta$-ambiguity of~\eqref{eq:beta}, because it is a property of the constraints and not of the solver. In our implementation, both networks carry the scale the way Cook \emph{et al.} describe for theirs (Sec.~\ref{sec:beta}): it is set on the first frame and then passed on by the warm start, each frame starting from the previous converged state. Unlike in~\cite{cook2011}, the first frame is set from the gyroscope instead of at random (Sec.~\ref{sec:deviations}). We leave the Cook network in this form. To the Martel network we add a reference for $\wvec$ that acts at every iteration, in three variants: a gyroscope anchor, a contrast-maximization anchor, and an interleaved update in which contrast maximization updates $\wvec$ directly.


\subsubsection{Gyroscope anchor}
We take Martel's idea of fusing a gyroscope (Sec.~\ref{sec:beta}), but instead of clamping $\wvec$ to the reading we add a soft anchoring cost
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}_{\text{anchor}} \;=\; \|\wvec - \wvec_{\text{ext}}\|^2,
\qquad \wvec_{\text{ext}} = \wvec_{\text{gyro}}.
\label{eq:anchorcost}
\end{equation}
At every iteration it moves $\wvec$ towards the reference,
\begin{equation}
\Delta \wvec \;=\; -\,\delta_{\text{anchor}}\,\big(\wvec - \wvec_{\text{ext}}\big),
\label{eq:anchor}
\end{equation}
alongside the updates of all the other costs. Because the reference has a definite scale and sign, this cost is not invariant under~\eqref{eq:beta}: it pulls $\wvec$ towards the scale and sign of the reference instead of leaving them free. Unlike a clamp, the anchor leaves the kinematics cost free to update $\wvec$ from the flow field as well, so the network can correct an imperfect reference; the weight $\delta_{\text{anchor}}$ sets how closely $\wvec$ follows it. We call this variant \texttt{thesis+IMU}.
 
\subsubsection{The contrast-maximization estimator}
\label{sec:coupling}
The gyroscope, however, is a second sensor. Any estimator whose objective is not invariant under~\eqref{eq:beta} can take its place. Contrast maximization~\cite{gallego2018cmax} is such an estimator: it warps the events of a window along the motion that a candidate $\wvec$ implies and chooses the $\wvec$ that makes the resulting image of warped events (IWE) sharpest, measured by its variance. A wrong hypothesis blurs the IWE whether its direction or its magnitude is wrong, so the objective determines $\wvec$ including its scale and sign, which the constraints~\eqref{eq:ofce}--\eqref{eq:kin} cannot. We use a Python port of the angular-velocity estimator of CMax-SLAM~\cite{guo2024cmaxslam}, run on the rectified events of one frame at a time, with each event voting with its polarity instead of with weight one, and couple it to the Martel network in two ways.

\paragraph{Per-frame anchor}
The first coupling, \texttt{CMax-anchor}, keeps the anchoring cost and only replaces the gyroscope by the estimator. Once per frame, before the message passing, the estimator is run to convergence on that frame's events, and its result $\wvec_{\text{cmax}}$ becomes the reference $\wvec_{\text{ext}}$ of~\eqref{eq:anchor} at every iteration of that frame. The kinematics cost still updates $\wvec$ from the flow field~\eqref{eq:normaleq}, so $\wvec$ is set jointly by the anchor and the network. Instead of the conjugate-gradient optimiser of CMax-SLAM, the estimator here uses a derivative-free simplex search (Nelder--Mead) on the IWE blurred by a Gaussian of $\sigma = 1$\,px.

\paragraph{Interleaved update}
The second coupling, \texttt{CMax-inloop}, drops the anchoring cost and lets contrast maximization update $\wvec$ directly. The kinematics cost now only propagates $\wvec$ into the flow ($\Fmap \leftarrow \Cmat\wvec$) and no longer updates $\wvec$. Instead, each message-passing iteration takes one gradient-ascent step on the contrast objective, written for the network's $R = \wvec\Delta t$,
\begin{equation}
R \;\leftarrow\; R \;+\; \lambda\,
  \frac{\partial}{\partial R}\operatorname{Var}\!\big[\mathrm{IWE}(R/\Delta t)\big].
\label{eq:inloop}
\end{equation}
The gradient is computed analytically on the unblurred IWE, as in the original CMax-SLAM. We use a fixed $\lambda = 10^{-4}$, at the upper end of the range we found stable. Because the gradient grows with the square of the number of events in the window, no single value suits all sequences.

The two couplings therefore differ in what sets the magnitude of $\wvec$: in \texttt{CMax-anchor} the kinematic constraint still acts on it, while in \texttt{CMax-inloop} contrast maximization alone determines it and the maps never correct it. 

\subsection{Development and Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

The code was written with the assistance of a large language model. Since the model has no reliable knowledge of either source, we did not ask for a method by name: we supplied the papers and the dissertation we implement, together with the dataset's file format, and asked for a skeleton of the two networks with the update rules transcribed from the numbered equations. The skeleton saved most of the time, but it was not correct. It silently substituted plausible variants for what the sources say, such as a centred difference where the paper specifies a forward one, a symmetric update where the paper's is asymmetric, or a normalisation the paper does not have, which give slightly wrong answers rather than errors. We therefore checked every update rule line by line against the equation it claims to implement, and treated anything not traceable to a numbered equation as our own decision, listed in Sec.~\ref{sec:deviations}. As a reader the model was useful, explaining derivation steps and catching several of our own errors, but it could not tell us whether a result was right.

That is what the validation is for. We built the system in layers (the Cook network, the Martel network, the anchoring cost, the lens distortion, contrast maximization) and validated each before adding the next. Because the output of the network is a single 3-vector that is hard to attribute to any one component, we relied on checks that isolate components rather than on end-to-end accuracy alone. 

The distortion-aware matrix~\eqref{eq:cdist} is our own derivation, so we checked it with the network removed, where the scale ambiguity cannot confound the comparison, using contrast maximization as the estimator. The forward distortion model matches OpenCV's to $2.8\times10^{-14}$\,px, and at a fixed rotation, warping the raw events the distortion-aware way and then undistorting the result lands on the pinhole warp of the rectified events to $0.043$\,px: the two routes of Sec.~\ref{sec:input} are the same map. 

The analytic gradient of the contrast objective was checked against finite differences. The contrast-maximization estimator was validated against \emph{both} the gyroscope and the motion-capture reference before being connected to the network at all, so that a later failure could be attributed to the coupling rather than to the estimator.

\subsubsection{Sequences and segments}
\label{sec:segments}
Both formulations model a camera that only rotates, in front of a static scene~\cite{cook2011,martel2015relational,martel2019thesis}, so we evaluate on rotation sequences, three recorded and two synthetic (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}).

The recorded ones are \texttt{boxes\_rotation}, \texttt{poster\_rotation} and \texttt{dynamic\_rotation} of the Event-Camera Dataset (ECD)~\cite{mueggler2017}, taken with a DAVIS240C ($240\times180$). The dataset provides OptiTrack motion-capture poses for the angular velocity, independent of the camera's gyroscope, and the camera's APS frames for the intensity. In \texttt{dynamic\_rotation} a person moves; we use a segment in which the person is seated.

The synthetic ones, \texttt{bicycle} and \texttt{street} of ECRot~\cite{guo2024cmaxslam}, are rendered for a model of the same sensor by rotating a virtual camera inside a panoramic photograph. The scene is therefore static and the simulated lens has no distortion, so~\eqref{eq:cdist} reduces to the pinhole form; the simulator records the exact angular velocity, which its simulated gyroscope matches to about $0.1^\circ$/s, and renders intensity frames in place of the APS frames. The two share one trajectory through different scenes, \texttt{street} with nearly three times as many events, so a difference between their results can only come from the scene. Their motion is faster than on the real sequences, steadier, about all three axes rather than mainly one, and does not reverse within the $1.5$\,s we score.

Each sequence is evaluated on a segment of $150$ frames of $\Delta t = 20$\,ms ($3$\,s); unless stated otherwise, the models run on its first $75$ frames ($1.5$\,s). Within these, the rotation of every real segment already reverses about its dominant axis, so a model must follow the motion to score well. Over all $150$ frames, the models with a reference keep the same order in angular-velocity error. On \texttt{boxes} and \texttt{poster} we report the segment of median speed among those we extracted, a choice made without regard to any model's error. Seven of these segments, from $0.42$ to $1.67$\,rad/s, are run over all $150$ frames to test whether the scale of the estimate depends on the speed (Sec.~\ref{sec:maps_add}).

\subsubsection{Angular velocity}
The angular velocity a model estimates at each frame is compared with a
reference angular velocity for the same frame, which comes from the datasets.
The simulator of the synthetic sequences records the exact angular velocity.
ECD records instead the orientation of the camera, measured $200$ times per
second by the motion-capture system, and we compute the angular velocity from
two of these orientations: the rotation from the first to the second, expressed
in the frame of the camera like the estimate, divided by the time between them.
Motion capture is a different instrument from the camera's gyroscope, so scoring
\texttt{thesis+IMU}, which uses the gyroscope, against it is not circular.

The two orientations are taken $50$\,ms before and after the middle of each
frame rather than at its start and end. Over a single $20$\,ms frame the camera
turns so little that the noise of the two orientations dominates: against such a
reference even the gyroscope scores $10$--$19^\circ$/s, against $6$--$9^\circ$/s
over the $\pm50$\,ms. On the synthetic sequences we average the exact angular
velocity over the same $\pm50$\,ms, so that both kinds of data are scored in the
same way. Every table reports the error of the gyroscope against the reference
as a noise floor: $6$--$9^\circ$/s on the real sequences and $0.68^\circ$/s on
the synthetic ones (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}).

Each frame is scored by three numbers: the error
$\|\wvec_{\text{est}}-\wvec_{\text{ref}}\|$ in $^\circ$/s; the direction error,
the angle between the two vectors; and the scale factor
$\beta = \|\wvec_{\text{ref}}\| / \|\wvec_{\text{est}}\|$, which is $1$ at the
correct speed and above $1$ when the speed is underestimated. $\beta$ is
reported separately because the rescaling~\eqref{eq:beta} changes the magnitude
and the sign of $\wvec$ but not its axis. The tables give the mean of each score
over the $75$ frames of a run, unless they state otherwise: a single frame says
little about a model, since the scores change considerably as the rotation
speeds up, slows down and reverses, whereas the mean covers the whole motion.



\subsubsection{Intensity}
\label{sec:recon_metric}
$\Imap$ is never observed, so we score it against the APS frame nearest in time, whose intensities, scaled to $[0,1]$, enter as $A = \log(1+\text{APS})$. The score is the magnitude $|r|$ of their Pearson correlation over the interior $80\%$ of the image, $r = \operatorname{cov}(\Imap, A)/(\sigma_{\Imap}\,\sigma_A)$. $r$ is $1$ when $\Imap$ equals $A$ up to a positive factor and an offset, $-1$ when the factor is negative, and near $0$ when the two are linearly unrelated. $|r|$ is therefore invariant to scale, offset and sign, which the reconstruction cannot determine: the $\beta$-ambiguity of Sec.~\ref{sec:beta} admits an inverted $\Imap$, and the mean of $\Imap$ is the undetermined constant of integration of~\eqref{eq:poisson_exact}.

 
The metrics usual for event-based reconstruction are MSE (or PSNR), SSIM~\cite{wang2004ssim} and LPIPS~\cite{zhang2018lpips}; works that apply them against the APS frames of the same dataset first equalise the histograms of both images~\cite{rebecq2019e2vid,zhang2023formulating}. Those works aim at images that look like photographs and compare them with other reconstruction methods, so they need to measure absolute intensity, local structure and perceived similarity. We only compare our five models with one another, for which $|r|$ needs no equalisation: on each frame it orders reconstructions as MSE would after the best affine fit of $\Imap$ to $A$, since the error left after that fit is $\sigma_A^2(1-r^2)$~\cite{freedman2007statistics}. It does not, however, measure local structure or perceived quality.

%The metrics usual for event-based reconstruction, MSE (or PSNR), SSIM~\cite{wang2004ssim} and LPIPS~\cite{zhang2018lpips}, compare absolute intensities: works that use them against the APS frames of the same dataset first equalise the histograms of both images, so that their intensity ranges are comparable~\cite{rebecq2019e2vid,zhang2023formulating}. $|r|$ needs no such step. On each frame it also orders reconstructions as MSE and PSNR would after the best affine fit of $\Imap$ to that frame, since the error remaining after that fit is $\sigma^2(1-r^2)$, with $\sigma^2$ the variance of the frame~\cite{freedman2007statistics}. 

Like the scores of the angular velocity, $|r|$ is computed at every frame and averaged over the $75$ frames, unless a table states otherwise. It stays well below unity even for a good map, since the APS frames differ from $\Imap$ in exposure and are offset in time.


\subsubsection{Configuration}
\label{sec:objective}

matrix~\eqref{eq:cdist} (Sec.~\ref{sec:distortion}). A few comparisons also use the step sizes we started from, $\delta_{F\!R} = 0.5$ and $\delta_{\text{anchor}} = 0.3$, to show what replacing them changed; the text

\section{Results on Angular Velocity}
\label{sec:experiments}

This section reports the angular velocity estimated by the five models, scored as described in Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}. It first compares the five models (Sec.~\ref{sec:mainresults}), then measures what the maps contribute once an anchor is present (Sec.~\ref{sec:maps_add}) and how the choice of parameters affects the result (Sec.~\ref{sec:sensitivity}).

\subsection{Comparison of the Five Models}
\label{sec:mainresults}
\label{sec:beta_exp}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports every model on the five sequences at the configuration of Table~\ref{tab:params}, and Fig.~\ref{fig:tracking} shows one run in detail. The three real sequences behave alike throughout, so we discuss the real and the synthetic sequences in turn.

Without a reference for $\wvec$, neither network tracks. On the real sequences the angular velocity of \texttt{cook} and \texttt{thesis} hardly moves after the first frame: over the $1.5$\,s its magnitude stays within $25\%$ of its first-frame value and its direction within $10^\circ$, while the reference turns its axis by up to $178^\circ$ and varies its speed between $0.06$ and $1.56$\,rad/s. \texttt{cook} keeps the gyroscope reading it starts from, and \texttt{thesis} shrinks it by $5$--$18\%$ in the first frame and then keeps it. Fig.~\ref{fig:tracking} shows the result, a flat trace through a full reversal of $\omega_x$. The direction errors of $76$--$85^\circ$ and total errors of $43$--$57^\circ$/s follow from the motion, not from a drift of the estimate. Over the first $0.5$\,s, before the rotation reverses, the same estimates score $14$--$24^\circ$/s and $6$--$20^\circ$ in direction, only because the gyroscope reading they start from still fits the motion. Each frame starts from the previous converged state, and the constraints of a frame can apparently be met by adapting the maps to the new events instead, so nothing forces $\wvec$ to follow the motion. The two update schemes fail alike here, the error of \texttt{cook} being $5$--$10\%$ above that of \texttt{thesis}.

On the synthetic sequences the first frame already shrinks the angular velocity, to about half of the gyroscope reading in \texttt{thesis} and to under a third in \texttt{cook}. \texttt{thesis} then keeps that value ($\beta = 2.6$ and $2.5$), whereas \texttt{cook} keeps shrinking it, to under $2\%$ of its first value by the last frame ($\beta = 181$ and $325$), while its maps remain mutually consistent. That one model ends at two such different scales on two sequences rendered along the same trajectory is the $\beta$-ambiguity in its purest form; Sec.~\ref{sec:converge} returns to the difference between the two schemes. The direction error of \texttt{thesis} is lower here (below $30^\circ$) than on real sequences, but not because it tracks: its estimate stays nearly constant, so its direction error is roughly the angle by which the true axis has turned since the first frame, at most $66^\circ$ on the synthetic segments against up to $178^\circ$ on the real ones. In both groups, a reference given once at the start does not anchor the model, whereas the same reference in the cost at every iteration does.

On the real sequences, \texttt{thesis+IMU} and \texttt{CMax-anchor} recover the rotation axis to $6$--$13^\circ$ and bring $\beta$ to within $14\%$ of unity. The error falls, against \texttt{thesis} without an anchor, by a factor of six to eight with the gyroscope as anchor and of $4.5$ to $6.7$ with the contrast estimate. Most of what the anchor provides therefore does not depend on where it comes from, but on total error the two are not interchangeable: the gyroscope is ahead by $1.4^\circ$/s on \texttt{boxes}, $2.4$ on \texttt{poster} and $2.5$ on \texttt{dynamic}. 

Part of each gap is a single frame. On each real segment the camera nearly stops before the rotation reverses, so that few events arrive, and on the worst of these frames, at $0.08$ to $0.20$\,rad/s, the error of \texttt{CMax-anchor} jumps to $122$, $54$ and $50^\circ$/s, while the gyroscope-anchored network stays at $3$--$5^\circ$/s: the contrast estimate fails for one frame and the network follows its anchor. Without that frame the two anchors are level on \texttt{boxes} ($5.7^\circ$/s with the contrast anchor, $5.9$ with the gyroscope), and the gyroscope stays ahead by $1.7$ and $1.9^\circ$/s on \texttt{poster} and \texttt{dynamic}. On the fastest real segment we measured, three seconds of \texttt{poster} at $1.67$\,rad/s, the gap widens to $4.7^\circ$/s. The noise floor of the reference grows with the speed as well, from $5.5^\circ$/s on the slowest \texttt{poster} segment ($0.42$\,rad/s) to $11.6$ on that fastest one, and relative to the speed the error of \texttt{CMax-anchor} falls, from $35\%$ to $17\%$ of $\|\wvec\|$.

We do not claim parity: an inertial measurement remains the better anchor. What the comparison establishes is that the contrast estimate can stand in for it, within a few degrees per second of a dedicated sensor, on a quantity the network cannot determine at all on its own, and from the events the network already processes. The gaps between the two anchors are also small next to the noise floor of the reference, $6$--$9^\circ$/s, at which \texttt{thesis+IMU} sits on all three real sequences ($5.8$, $8.7$ and $7.0^\circ$/s, against $6.2$, $8.8$ and $7.2$ for its gyroscope). That it scores slightly below its own gyroscope shows that the visual constraints are not overridden by the inertial signal, not that they improve on it.

The synthetic sequences, whose reference is exact, resolve the error of the model itself. \texttt{CMax-anchor} reaches $7.1^\circ$/s against a floor of $0.68$, seven times less than without an anchor, with the axis recovered to about $2^\circ$, so almost all of its error is magnitude, $\beta = 1.09$. \texttt{thesis+IMU} does slightly better, but its simulated gyroscope is nearly exact, so anchoring on it amounts to handing the model its own answer, and we do not compare the two anchors there. The two synthetic sequences agree to within $0.2^\circ$/s and $0.02$ in $\beta$ despite their different scenes, so this error depends on the motion and the model rather than on what the camera sees; Sec.~\ref{sec:maps_add} shows where it comes from.

\texttt{CMax-inloop} is not a fusion of the two estimates: the maps never act on its $\wvec$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:coupling}), so it is contrast maximization run by gradient steps inside the network loop. On the real sequences its errors ($8.3$, $14.5$ and $11.3^\circ$/s) and its direction errors are above those of \texttt{CMax-anchor} on all three. On the synthetic pair it fails: its error reaches $45^\circ$/s on \texttt{bicycle}, and on \texttt{street}, the denser of the two, it diverges. This is consistent with how its step on $\wvec$ scales: the gradient grows with the square of the number of events in the window, so a step size that is stable on one sequence can be too large on a denser one (Sec.~\ref{sec:coupling}).

Whether that helps depends on the anchor. Contrast maximization alone overestimates the speed on the real sequences and is nearly exact on the synthetic ones, so the same shrinkage corrects the one and spoils the other. At $\delta_{V\!F\!G} = 0.48$ the network improves on its anchor by $15$--$37\%$ on the real sequences, whereas on the synthetic ones the best it can do is return the anchor unchanged. The synthetic columns of Table~\ref{tab:main} therefore show what the method does when its anchor is already near-perfect, rather than a second, independent validation.

How strongly the maps shrink $\|\wvec\|$ is set by the balance between the kinematic update and the anchor. Both act on $\wvec$ in the same update, $\wvec \leftarrow \wvec - \delta_{F\!R}(\wvec-\wvec^\star) - \delta_{\text{anchor}}(\wvec-\wvec_{\text{ext}})$, with $\wvec^\star = M^{-1}v$ the least-squares estimate from the flow~\eqref{eq:normaleq}. For given $\wvec^\star$ and $\wvec_{\text{ext}}$, its fixed point depends only on the ratio $\kappa = \delta_{F\!R}/\delta_{\text{anchor}}$.

The data agree. In Table~\ref{tab:sweep} the rows $\delta_{\text{anchor}} = 0.1$ and $\delta_{F\!R} = 0.5$, which share $\kappa = 1$, agree to within $0.1^\circ$/s on every sequence, and as $\delta_{F\!R}$ grows from $0.02$ to $0.9$ the scale factor rises without exception on every sequence, from $0.93$ to $1.05$ on \texttt{boxes}, $0.85$ to $1.08$ on \texttt{poster} and $1.02$ to $1.51$ on \texttt{bicycle}. Since $\beta$ rises as $\wvec^\star$ gains weight, $\wvec^\star$ is slower than the anchor on every sequence. The constraints leave this open: the anchor constrains $\wvec$, but not how the rescaling~\eqref{eq:beta} splits $\Vmap$ between $\Fmap$ and $\Gmap$, and the relaxation settles that split from the data.

At a large $\kappa$ the shrinkage also depends on speed. Over the seven segments measured on \texttt{boxes} and \texttt{poster}, which span $0.42$ to $1.67$\,rad/s, $\beta$ of \texttt{CMax-anchor} at the step sizes we started from, $\delta_{F\!R} = 0.5$ and $\delta_{\text{anchor}} = 0.3$ ($\kappa = 1.7$), is strongly correlated with rotation speed ($r = +0.96$, $p < 0.001$). It rises from $0.80$ at the slowest segment to $1.18$ at the fastest, i.e.\ from a $25\%$ overestimate to a $15\%$ underestimate of $\|\wvec\|$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:beta_speed}). At the adopted $\kappa = 0.2$ the correlation falls to $+0.60$, no longer significant at $n = 7$, and $\beta$ stays within $0.79$--$0.97$ over the whole range of speeds.

The control is \texttt{CMax-inloop}, in which the kinematic cost does not update $\wvec$. Its $\wvec$ is identical, to the last digit, in every pair of runs we compared that differ only in the step sizes or the regularisation of the maps, so it cannot depend on $\kappa$, and its $\beta$ shows no comparable trend with speed ($r = -0.47$, not significant). The shrinkage therefore appears only where the kinematic update acts on $\wvec$. In exchange, \texttt{CMax-inloop} has the larger direction error on every segment, and at the adopted step sizes \texttt{CMax-anchor} has the lower total error on all seven.

Neither scene content nor speed explains the level of the synthetic sequences. The two, rendered along the same trajectory over scenes that differ in event density, give $\beta = 1.485$ and $1.482$ at the starting step sizes over the same three seconds, and at their $1.32$\,rad/s the trend fitted to the real segments at those step sizes predicts $\beta \approx 1.09$, against the $1.48$ measured. What sets them apart from the real sequences we cannot say.

\subsection{Sensitivity and Ablation}
\label{sec:sensitivity}
The parameters were examined in two stages, both with \texttt{CMax-anchor} and over $1.5$\,s on all five sequences. First, $\delta_{F\!R}$ and $\delta_{\text{anchor}}$ were varied together on a $5\times5$ grid, $\delta_{F\!R} \in \{0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5\}$ against $\delta_{\text{anchor}} \in \{0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9\}$; the other four rates had been set by hand early on and were held fixed. Second, every rate, the iteration count and the window were varied one at a time around the adopted values, the anchor weight was swept also for \texttt{thesis+IMU} (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}). \texttt{CMax-anchor} is the model in which both the maps and the contrast estimate act on $\wvec$, and without its anchor it is \texttt{thesis}. The other models were not swept: for the two networks without a reference, our results hold at the step sizes of Table~\ref{tab:params}. This subsection reports what the grid shows, the effect of removing the anchor, the parameters that matter little and the accumulation window.

\subsubsection{Choice of the step sizes}
\label{sec:realvssynth}
On the grid, as in Table~\ref{tab:sweep}, settings with equal $\kappa$ give the same error to within $0.1^\circ$/s, so the grid is in effect a one-dimensional scan of $\kappa$. Along it the two kinds of data want opposite things. Each real sequence is best at a moderate $\kappa$: $0.4$--$0.5$ on \texttt{boxes}, $0.6$ on \texttt{poster} and $1$ on \texttt{dynamic}, with $6.9$, $9.0$ and $8.4^\circ$/s. On the synthetic ones the error falls monotonically as $\kappa$ goes to zero, i.e.\ as the network is reduced to its anchor, to $2.7^\circ$/s on both at the smallest $\kappa$ tested.

The adopted $\kappa = 0.2$ ($\delta_{F\!R} = 0.1$, $\delta_{\text{anchor}} = 0.5$) lies between the two and is the optimum of neither. It was not taken from the grid but from an earlier one-at-a-time sweep around the values we began with ($\delta_{F\!R} = 0.5$, $\delta_{\text{anchor}} = 0.3$), in which the smallest $\delta_{F\!R}$ and the largest $\delta_{\text{anchor}}$ tested, $0.1$ and $0.5$, each lowered the error on all five sequences. A $\kappa$ near $0.6$ would lower the error on all three real sequences, by $3$ to $18\%$, but would more than double it on the synthetic ones. Relative to the starting values ($\kappa = 1.7$), the adopted pair lowers the mean error on both kinds of data, from $9.5$ to $9.3^\circ$/s on the real sequences and from $25.8$ to $7.1^\circ$/s on the synthetic ones, though not on every sequence: on \texttt{dynamic} it rises from $8.7$ to $9.6^\circ$/s.

The one-at-a-time sweep shows the same split on every rate that matters. $\delta_{F\!R}$, $\delta_{\text{anchor}}$, $\delta_{V\!F\!G}$ and $\delta_{R\!F}$ each move the error substantially, and for each the real and the synthetic sequences are best on opposite sides of the adopted value, so no single configuration is best for both. All four set how much authority over $\wvec$ the maps hold against the anchor: a small $\delta_{F\!R}$ or $\delta_{V\!F\!G}$, a large $\delta_{\text{anchor}}$, or a large $\delta_{R\!F}$, which makes the flow follow the anchored $\wvec$, all hand it to the anchor, which helps on the synthetic sequences and harms on the real ones (Sec.~\ref{sec:maps_add}).

\subsubsection{Anchor ablation}
Setting $\delta_{\text{anchor}} = 0$ removes the anchoring cost~\eqref{eq:anchorcost} and leaves the Martel network without a reference; the row reproduces \texttt{thesis} of Table~\ref{tab:main}, and it is the same model for both anchors. Nothing else in the sweep comes close to its effect. At every non-zero weight the two anchors stay within $3.5^\circ$/s of each other, against $43$--$54^\circ$/s with no anchor, and the gyroscope stays ahead everywhere except at the weakest weight on \texttt{poster}. 

Among the non-zero weights the two kinds of data part ways again. On the synthetic sequences a stronger anchor is better throughout, for both anchors ($20.5$ to $4.7$--$4.8^\circ$/s from $0.1$ to $0.9$ with the contrast anchor). On the real ones the contrast anchor is best at $0.1$--$0.3$ and worse beyond (\texttt{poster} $9.4^\circ$/s at $0.1$, $12.1$ at $0.9$), while the gyroscope anchor changes by at most $0.3^\circ$/s anywhere from $0.3$ to $0.9$.

\subsubsection{Parameters with little effect}
\label{sec:other_deltas}
$\delta_{I\!G}$ and $\delta_{G\!I}$, the two rates that couple $\Imap$ and $\Gmap$, move the error by at most $0.5^\circ$/s on any sequence, across a thirteen-fold and a ten-fold change: $\wvec$ sees the exchange between $\Imap$ and $\Gmap$ only at second hand, through the flow constraint. Their effect on the reconstruction is larger. Raising $\delta_{G\!I}$ from $0.02$ to $0.2$ raises the mean $|r|$ of the read-out on all five sequences, from $0.27$ to $0.34$ on average.

Iterations per frame matter even less. Across $25$ to $150$ the error of \texttt{CMax-anchor} changes by at most $0.2^\circ$/s on any sequence, and the $|r|$ of its read-out by at most $0.05$. Only the in-loop map gains from more iterations: its $|r|$ rises with the iteration count on every sequence, on \texttt{boxes} from $0.14$ at $25$ iterations to $0.21$ at $75$ and $0.26$ at $150$. For the angular velocity and the read-out of \texttt{CMax-anchor}, the $50$--$75$ of~\cite{martel2019thesis} can therefore be reduced to $25$; for the in-loop map they cannot. 

\subsubsection{Accumulation window}
\label{sec:window_sweep}
For the angular velocity, a longer window is better across the whole range tested and on every sequence: the mean error is $34.8^\circ$/s at $5$\,ms, $14.3$ at $10$ ms, $9.3$ at $20$ ms and $7.6$ at $40$ ms on the real sequences, and $33.0$, $7.1$ and $6.9$ at $10$, $20$ and $40$\,ms on the synthetic ones, which at $5$\,ms do not track at all ($\beta$ of order $10^4$). A shorter window gives the contrast estimate that anchors $\wvec$ fewer events per frame, and $\wvec = R/\Delta t$ divides whatever error remains in $R$ by a smaller number. The degradation is not an artefact of how we score: the synthetic sequences are scored against the simulator's exact angular velocity, with no pose differencing involved, and degrade the most.

For the reconstruction the ordering reverses, as a longer window smears each edge over more pixels of $\Vmap$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:reconstruction}). The mean $|r|$ of the read-out falls from $0.49$ at $10$\,ms to $0.30$ at $20$ and $0.12$ at $40$ on the real sequences ($0.51$, $0.23$ and $0.14$ on the synthetic ones). A single window cannot serve both. This report keeps $20$\,ms, which costs the angular velocity $1.7^\circ$/s against $40$\,ms on the real sequences but keeps more than twice the $|r|$; a system built for the image should use a different configuration (Sec.~\ref{sec:image_settings}).

\section{Results on Intensity Reconstruction}
\label{sec:recon_experiments}
Besides $\wvec$, the network infers the maps $\Imap$, $\Gmap$ and $\Fmap$. Figure~\ref{fig:maps} shows them for \texttt{CMax-anchor} on all five sequences, at the last frame of its runs in Table~\ref{tab:main}, with one exception explained below; Appendix~\ref{app:maps} shows the same frames for the other four models. The network gives the intensity in two forms. The \emph{in-loop} map is the $\Imap$ that the network carries and updates by~\eqref{eq:cook_poisson} at every iteration; the \emph{read-out} is the image recovered from the final $\Gmap$ by the exact solve~\eqref{eq:poisson_exact} (Sec.~\ref{sec:deviations}). The $|r|$ of Table~\ref{tab:main} scores the read-out.

On the three real sequences both intensity maps show a recognisable scene, although the network receives nothing but the events. The gradient map is concentrated on the edges of the scene. This holds for \texttt{dynamic\_rotation} too. Its scene contains a person, and a person who moves breaks the model's assumption of a static scene; in the segment we use, however, the person is seated and the scene nearly static (Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}). Its reconstruction scores $|r| = 0.29$, between those of the other two real sequences ($0.41$ and $0.19$). On the two synthetic sequences a diagonal ripple covers much of the image and the scene shows through only in part; the ripple appears only in the anchored models (Sec.~\ref{sec:anchor_image}). On every sequence the flow map varies smoothly across the image, as the flow of a pure rotation does.

The quality of $\Imap$ also changes from frame to frame. In the anchored models the intensity map has \emph{excursions}: stretches of frames in which its values grow far beyond their usual range and the scene is lost. We count a frame as part of an excursion when the standard deviation of $\Imap$ over the image exceeds five times its median over the $75$ frames. For \texttt{CMax-anchor} on \texttt{poster} this median is $0.10$. In frames $56$--$68$, which begin in the fastest part of the rotation, the standard deviation rises above five times that value, to a peak of $1.59$. The same network without an anchor, \texttt{thesis}, stays between $0.02$ and $0.05$ throughout. The angular velocity does not degrade during the excursion: on its $13$ frames the error of \texttt{CMax-anchor} is $11.2^\circ$/s, against $11.0$ on the other $62$.

The last frames of \texttt{poster} come just after this excursion, before the map has recovered. Figure~\ref{fig:maps} therefore shows \texttt{poster} at frame $43$, where $|r|$ is highest of the $75$ frames, both for \texttt{CMax-anchor} ($0.75$, against a mean of $0.19$) and averaged over the five models.

The rest of the section looks at how $\Imap$ forms and what limits its quality. Sec.~\ref{sec:converge} follows $\Imap$ through the iterations of one frame. Sec.~\ref{sec:reconstruction} identifies the factors its quality depends on, Sec.~\ref{sec:anchor_image} shows that the anchor, which the angular velocity needs, degrades the image, and Sec.~\ref{sec:regularisers} tests three terms that stabilise the maps. Finally, Sec.~\ref{sec:image_settings} sets the configuration for the image instead of for $\wvec$.

\subsection{Convergence Within a Frame}
\label{sec:converge}

The maps of Fig.~\ref{fig:maps} are the state the network reaches after $75$ iterations of a frame; the two update schemes differ in how they get there. Figure~\ref{fig:converge} follows the intensity map through the iterations of a single frame, with $\Imap$ initialised from noise at that frame rather than carried over from the previous one ($\Fmap$ and $\wvec$ start from the gyroscope reading at the beginning of the segment). Nothing in the model computes $\Imap$ from $\Vmap$ directly, yet the scene assembles itself over a few tens of iterations, and by iteration $75$ the desk, the monitor and the objects on the desk are legible.

The sequential scheme of Cook \emph{et al.} has recognisable structure after a \emph{single} iteration, because each sub-update immediately sees the output of the one before it, so information crosses the whole network within one sweep. The simultaneous scheme is still noise at iterations one and three and only develops structure by about ten, because every message is computed from a common snapshot and therefore travels one relation per iteration. By iteration $75$ the two are comparable, consistent with their close scores on the real sequences in Table~\ref{tab:main}: on real data the two schemes differ in how fast they converge rather than in the state they reach.

Speed is not free. On the synthetic sequence \texttt{bicycle} the sequential scheme is also the less stable of the two: it produces a good map at the first iterations and then diverges, collapsing to an almost flat field by iteration ten, while all four simultaneous variants converge normally on the same input (Appendix~\ref{app:converge}). On this input, then, the simultaneous scheme is also the more stable one; the thesis adopts it for a different reason, its parallelism (Sec.~\ref{sec:martel}).

\subsection{Factors Affecting the Reconstruction}
\label{sec:reconstruction}

The intensity map is where our results looked least like the published ones. Martel and Cook show intensity maps that resemble photographs, whereas our in-loop maps often resembled a relief of the scene edges, as in the columns of Fig.~\ref{fig:recon} labelled iterative. This subsection identifies two causes, the accumulation window and the way $\Imap$ is computed from $\Gmap$; the next two subsections add the anchor (Sec.~\ref{sec:anchor_image}) and the regularisation of the maps (Sec.~\ref{sec:regularisers}). The comparison of intensity updates and one of the regularising terms depend on a property of $\Gmap$, its integrability, which we introduce first.

\subsubsection{Integrability of the gradient map}
\label{sec:curl}

Recovering $\Imap$ from $\Gmap$ assumes that $\Gmap$ is the gradient of some image. A vector field is a gradient only if it is curl-free, $\partial_y\Gmap^{(x)} = \partial_x\Gmap^{(y)}$; equivalently, its sum around any closed path is zero, since the path ends at the pixel it started from, which has a single intensity.

Nothing in the model enforces this. Two costs act on $\Gmap$: the spatial cost pulls it towards $\nabla\Imap$, which is curl-free, while the flow constraint~\eqref{eq:ofce} acts on each pixel separately and links no neighbouring pixels. What the two settle on is in general not curl-free. The aperture problem adds to this: \eqref{eq:ofce} fixes only the component of $\Gmap$ along $\Fmap$, so the component perpendicular to $\Fmap$ can drift. The exact solve~\eqref{eq:poisson_exact} returns the image whose gradient is closest to $\Gmap$, and so discards the curl: the curl is the part of $\Gmap$ that no image can reproduce.

Splitting $\Gmap$ into a curl-free and a divergence-free part measures how large that part is. In the final $\Gmap$ of the \texttt{CMax-anchor} runs of Table~\ref{tab:main}, the curl holds $3$--$20\%$ of the energy on the real sequences and $18$--$29\%$ on the synthetic ones.

This curl share is not a measure of quality. At $\Delta t = 5$\,ms it is below $1\%$ on the synthetic sequences,  where both the angular velocity (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}) and the image ($|r| = 0.06$) fail: at that window $\Gmap$ hardly forms, and a field close to zero has almost no curl. Nor does it grow steadily with the window: on \texttt{poster} it is $5$, $8$, $20$ and $6\%$ at $5$, $10$, $20$ and $40$\,ms. We therefore compare it only between runs at the same window.

\subsubsection{Accumulation window}

The event frame $\Vmap$ collects the events of a window $\Delta t$, during which the image moves. At the $20$\,ms used throughout this report, the image centre moves by $3.4$\,px per window on the real sequences and $5.0$\,px on the synthetic ones, whereas $\Gmap$ compares neighbouring pixels. An edge is therefore smeared over several pixels of $\Vmap$, and $\Gmap$ inherits the smear. In Fig.~\ref{fig:recon} this shows as streaks along the direction of motion, vertical on \texttt{boxes}, whose rotation is mainly about the horizontal axis and so moves the image up and down.

Halving the window to $10$\,ms halves the smear and improves the reconstruction (Table~\ref{tab:recon}). The mean $|r|$ of the read-out rises from $0.28$ to $0.55$, higher on all $12$ runs, and that of the in-loop map from $0.13$ to $0.28$, higher on $11$. Since both improve, the gain comes from $\Gmap$ itself. It differs between sequences: in the frames of Fig.~\ref{fig:recon} the in-loop map of \texttt{boxes}, with its vertical streaks, rises from $0.35$ to $0.48$, while that of \texttt{poster}, whose artefacts have no dominant direction, stays at $0.42$ and $0.41$. Doubling the window to $40$\,ms has the opposite effect, and a shorter window raises the angular-velocity error, which is why the configuration keeps $20$\,ms as a compromise (Sec.~\ref{sec:window_sweep}).

\subsubsection{Intensity update}

From the same $\Gmap$, the exact solve~\eqref{eq:poisson_exact} gives a better image than the in-loop map, because it recovers the low spatial frequencies that the gradient step~\eqref{eq:cook_poisson} approaches only slowly (Sec.~\ref{sec:martel}). Consistent with this, more iterations per frame improve the in-loop map on every sequence but leave the read-out nearly unchanged (Sec.~\ref{sec:other_deltas}). The read-out scores higher on $10$ of the $12$ runs at $20$\,ms and on $11$ of $12$ at $10$\,ms (Table~\ref{tab:recon}). In Fig.~\ref{fig:recon} this is the difference between a relief and an image: the stones of \texttt{poster} gain flat interiors, and in the $10$\,ms frame of \texttt{boxes} the read-out reaches $|r| = 0.84$, with the log pile, the boxes and the calibration target all legible.

The exact solve cannot simply replace~\eqref{eq:cook_poisson} inside the loop. There it amplifies the low-frequency error of $\Gmap$ by $\lvert k\rvert^{-2}$ at every iteration, and $\Imap$ fills with large spurious blobs. We measure this by the low-frequency share of $\Imap$, the fraction of its Fourier amplitude below $10$ cycles per image. Over $3$\,s of \texttt{CMax-anchor} on \texttt{poster}, the cosine transform in the loop leaves one smooth blob across the image: the low-frequency share of $\Imap$ is $0.40$, against $0.16$ for the APS frame and $0.11$ with the gradient step. The Fourier transform instead puts $\Imap$ in an excursion on $31\%$ of the frames, against $12\%$ with the gradient step. On \texttt{bicycle} both make $\Imap$ six times more variable. The average quality of the in-loop map is mixed: its $|r|$ is $0.21$ with the cosine and $0.41$ with the Fourier transform on \texttt{poster}, and $0.19$ and $0.11$ on \texttt{bicycle}, against $0.30$ and $0.15$ for the read-out of the network with the gradient step. What rules the exact solve out of the loop is therefore its instability. Applied once per frame as a read-out, its errors cannot feed back into the network, so we use~\eqref{eq:cook_poisson} in the loop and~\eqref{eq:poisson_exact} for the read-out.

For the read-out, the two transforms we implemented, the Fourier and the cosine one (Sec.~\ref{sec:deviations}), remain to be compared, and here the measurement contradicts the expectation. The cosine transform matches the border convention of our forward differences, and on a test field that is the exact gradient of an image it recovers the image exactly (relative RMS error $2\!\times\!10^{-13}$), whereas the Fourier transform is off by a ramp that wraps around the border (relative RMS error $0.63$). On the $\Gmap$ of the network, however, the Fourier transform scores higher on average at both windows: $0.28$ against $0.25$ at $20$\,ms, where it wins $7$ of the $12$ runs, essentially a tie, and $0.55$ against $0.31$ at $10$\,ms, where it wins $11$ of $12$.

One explanation fits both observations, although we have not tested it directly. The test field is a valid gradient field, for which the cosine transform is exact. The $\Gmap$ of the network is not: it carries curl (Sec.~\ref{sec:curl}) and low-frequency error, which the exact solve amplifies by $\lvert k\rvert^{-2}$. The wrap-around of the Fourier transform may suppress part of that error, whereas the cosine transform integrates it as faithfully as the signal, so the more accurate solver gives the worse image. We report the Fourier read-out.

The choice of intensity update does not change the angular velocity. On the same $3$\,s of \texttt{poster}, the error is $11.32^\circ$/s with the gradient step in the loop and $11.31$ and $11.33$ with the Fourier and the cosine solve, although the low-frequency share of $\Imap$ almost quadruples ($0.11$ to $0.40$). This is consistent with how the model is coupled: $\Imap$ reaches $\wvec$ only through $\Gmap$ and the flow, and at the adopted $\kappa = 0.2$ the fixed point of $\wvec$, $(\kappa\wvec^\star + \wvec_{\text{ext}})/(1+\kappa)$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:maps_add}), weights the anchor five times more than the estimate from the flow.

\subsection{Anchoring and Image Quality}
\label{sec:anchor_image}

The anchor, which the angular velocity needs (Sec.~\ref{sec:mainresults}), degrades the intensity map. The excursions of $\Imap$ described at the start of this section occur only with an anchor: no run without an anchor has an excursion frame on any sequence, while the anchored models have up to $13$ of $75$ on the real sequences. On the synthetic sequences they have none, and their maps show the ripple of Fig.~\ref{fig:maps} instead. A likely reason is that the anchor makes the constraints inconsistent: it holds $\wvec$, and with it $\Fmap$, away from $\wvec^\star$, the estimate the flow alone would give, which is slower than the anchor on every sequence (Sec.~\ref{sec:maps_add}). The intensity map would then absorb the disagreement.

Across the models, the better image comes with the worse angular velocity. Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation} shows the last frame of \texttt{boxes}, \texttt{bicycle} and \texttt{street}, reconstructed by the network without an anchor (\texttt{thesis}) and by the two anchored ones. Without an anchor $|r|$ is $0.63$, $0.60$ and $0.56$, and the synthetic scenes are legible down to the shop signs; with an anchor it is $0.41$--$0.45$, $0.13$ and $0.32$--$0.33$, and the synthetic scenes are covered by the ripple. The angular-velocity error goes the opposite way: $49$--$52^\circ$/s without an anchor, $5.8$--$7.3$ with one. \texttt{poster} shows the same pattern ($0.53$ against $0.19$--$0.26$, Table~\ref{tab:main}); only on \texttt{dynamic} are the images level ($0.36$ against $0.29$--$0.35$). The gap is smaller over longer runs and shorter windows. Over $3$\,s instead of $1.5$, the read-out of \texttt{thesis} falls on four of the five sequences, on \texttt{boxes} from $0.63$ to $0.46$, while that of the anchored models rises on \texttt{boxes} and \texttt{poster}, and on \texttt{boxes} \texttt{thesis+IMU} ends ahead ($0.50$). At $10$\,ms the anchored network reconstructs about as well as the unanchored one (Table~\ref{tab:image})..

The image of the unanchored network is not entirely right either: its $\Imap$ is inverted whenever its $\wvec$ points away from the true one (Appendix~\ref{app:maps}). This is the sign ambiguity of~\eqref{eq:beta}, which $|r|$ deliberately ignores (Sec.~\ref{sec:recon_metric}).

\subsection{Regularisation of the Maps}
\label{sec:regularisers}

The excursions occur only with the anchor, and nothing in either source limits them, since neither regularises $\Gmap$. They most likely grow through the flow constraint~\eqref{eq:ofce}, the one relation that acts on each pixel separately: it can enlarge $\Gmap$ at a few pixels without any neighbour opposing it. Guo and Gallego report the same failure in their event-based mosaicing bundle adjustment~\cite{guo2024emba}, where each residual likewise depends on the map gradient at a single pixel, and add a shrinkage prior to suppress it.

We test three regularising terms. They come from different places, which matters for how much weight each result deserves:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{$\delta_{\text{shrink}I}$, on $\Imap$.} The leading $\Imap$ of
  the thesis' Eq.~6.61, which we omit (Sec.~\ref{sec:deviations}). Setting it to $1$ restores the published update exactly.
\item \textbf{$\delta_{\text{map}}$, on $\Gmap$.} The prior
  $\eta\lVert\Gmap\rVert^2$ of~\cite{guo2024emba}, giving
  $\Gmap \leftarrow (1-2\delta_{\text{map}})\Gmap$. Not in either source
  implemented here.
\item \textbf{$\delta_{\text{curl}}$, on $\Gmap$.} Ours. It removes the curl
  of Sec.~\ref{sec:curl}, the part of $\Gmap$ that no image can reproduce,
  by penalising $\lVert\Gmap - P\Gmap\rVert^2$, with $P$ the projection onto
  the curl-free fields, so a fraction $\delta_{\text{curl}}$ of the curl is
  removed each iteration.
\end{enumerate}

Table~\ref{tab:regularisers} reports all three; its last column is the fraction of frames spent in an excursion. No term is best on every measure: $\delta_{\text{curl}} = 0.6$ gives the lowest mean angular-velocity error and the best read-out but raises the error on \texttt{bicycle}, while $\delta_{\text{map}} = 0.03$ removes the excursions and gives the best in-loop map but does not improve the read-out.

The thesis' own term changes least. Restoring it leaves the angular velocity unchanged on the three real sequences and the read-out almost unchanged ($0.257$ to $0.262$); its one clear effect is on \texttt{bicycle}, whose error falls from $7.1$ to $6.7^\circ$/s. It also \emph{lowers} the quality of the in-loop map, from $0.149$ to $0.114$ on average and on each real sequence. Leaving it out (Sec.~\ref{sec:deviations}) therefore does not explain why our intensity maps looked unlike the published ones; the window and the read-out change $|r|$ far more (Sec.~\ref{sec:reconstruction}).

The prior of~\cite{guo2024emba} does what it was designed for, and no more. It removes the excursions completely: the fraction of frames spent in one falls from $0.063$ to $0$ on average, and from $0.17$ to $0$ on \texttt{poster}. It also gives the best in-loop map of any configuration tested ($0.212$). It does not improve the read-out ($0.257$ to $0.252$), and on \texttt{dynamic} it lowers it from $0.29$ to $0.26$, so the excursions are not what separates the read-out of the anchored network from that of the unanchored one (Sec.~\ref{sec:anchor_image}).

The curl term is the only one that improves the read-out: $|r|$ rises from $0.257$ to $0.353$ on average, and Table~\ref{tab:curl} shows it improving on all five sequences. It also lowers the angular-velocity error on the three real sequences, but raises it on both synthetic ones (from $7.1$ to $7.7^\circ$/s on \texttt{bicycle} and to $7.9$ on \texttt{street}, Table~\ref{tab:curl}), the same split between real and synthetic data as for the step sizes (Sec.~\ref{sec:realvssynth}).

Two cautions apply. The term is our own construction, proposed neither by the sources nor by the mosaicing work, and we have not validated it independently. And inside the network it does not do what it does on its own: applied to a fixed field it drives the curl to zero, but in the running network the curl share of the final $\Gmap$ falls below $1\%$ only on \texttt{boxes} and \texttt{dynamic}, and \emph{rises} on \texttt{poster} (from $20$ to $96\%$) and on the synthetic sequences (from $29$ to $56\%$ and from $18$ to $35\%$). We cannot explain this at present.

Figure~\ref{fig:regularisers} shows what the three terms do to the image on four sequences. The differences are small on \texttt{boxes}, where the reconstruction without a term is already good, and largest on \texttt{poster}, where only the curl term recovers the stones at all.

None of the three is adopted in the configuration of Table~\ref{tab:params}; all results elsewhere in this report are without them. We do not adopt $\delta_{\text{curl}}$, despite its gains, because it is our own, unvalidated addition and because it raises the angular-velocity error on the synthetic sequences. Table~\ref{tab:curl} gives the full comparison against Table~\ref{tab:main}, model by model.

\subsection{Image-Optimised Configuration}
\label{sec:image_settings}

All results so far use the configuration of Table~\ref{tab:params}, which was chosen for $\wvec$ alone (Sec.~\ref{sec:objective}); it remains the configuration behind every angular-velocity result in this report. This section identified three settings that matter for the image: the window, the curl term and the anchor. Here we set them for the image instead, trying every combination of $\Delta t \in \{20, 10\}$\,ms, $\delta_{\text{curl}} \in \{0, 0.6\}$ and \texttt{thesis} without an anchor or \texttt{CMax-anchor} with one, with every other parameter as in Table~\ref{tab:params}, on $1.5$\,s runs scored by the mean $|r|$ of the read-out (Table~\ref{tab:image}). The $20$\,ms runs are those of Tables~\ref{tab:main} and~\ref{tab:curl}. $\delta_{G\!I}$, which also raises the read-out at almost no cost to $\wvec$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:other_deltas}), is held at its adopted value. Since real and synthetic sequences already wanted opposite settings for $\wvec$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:realvssynth}), we choose the best configuration separately for each kind of data.

They want opposite settings for the image as well. On the real sequences the best image keeps the anchor: \texttt{CMax-anchor} at $10$\,ms with $\delta_{\text{curl}} = 0.6$ raises the mean $|r|$ from $0.30$ to $0.55$, while its angular-velocity error rises only from $9.3$ to $14.0^\circ$/s, so this configuration still tracks. On the synthetic sequences the best image drops the anchor: \texttt{thesis} at $20$\,ms with $\delta_{\text{curl}} = 0.6$ reaches $0.65$ against $0.23$, but at an angular-velocity error of $52.6^\circ$/s, which makes $\wvec$ useless.

The best window also depends on the anchor. Halving it improves the anchored network on all five sequences and degrades the unanchored one on all five; on the synthetic sequences it also raises the anchored error from $7.1$ to $33.0^\circ$/s, the cost of short windows for the angular velocity (Sec.~\ref{sec:window_sweep}). Figure~\ref{fig:image_settings} compares the two configurations: the stones of \texttt{poster}, barely visible in the configuration for $\wvec$, become legible, and so do the \texttt{bicycle} and \texttt{street} scenes, which the ripple covered.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We re-implemented the two published formulations of Interacting Maps, the sequential relaxation of Cook \emph{et al.} and the energy-based message passing of Martel's dissertation, on a shared camera model, and evaluated them on three real and two synthetic rotation sequences. To our knowledge no implementation of either was previously available and only a qualitative evaluation of both models was presented by the authors.

The clearest result is negative and concerns the method as published. Neither formulation produces a usable angular velocity: direction errors of $76$--$85^\circ$ on the real sequences mean the recovered axis is uninformative, and the magnitude can leave any plausible range entirely, reaching $\beta = 325$ on \texttt{street} while the maps remain mutually consistent (Table~\ref{tab:main}). Starting from the correct value does not prevent this. On the real sequences the estimate keeps the value of its first frame and does not follow the motion, apparently because the constraints of each new frame can be met by adapting the maps instead, and Gauss--Seidel and Jacobi fail in the same way, $5$--$10\%$ apart. On the synthetic sequences the Gauss--Seidel network also shrinks the estimate towards zero, the scale ambiguity of~\eqref{eq:beta} resolving itself arbitrarily, and it is the less stable of the two (Sec.~\ref{sec:converge}).

What makes the method work is an absolute reference inside the cost that is minimised at every iteration. Supplied by a gyroscope, as the thesis proposes, it reduces the error by six to eight times and brings the direction error to $1$--$10^\circ$. Our main positive result is that this reference need not be a measurement: a contrast-maximization estimate computed from the same events occupies the same place in the model and comes within $1.4$--$2.5^\circ$/s of the inertial anchor on the three real sequences, without a second sensor. Part of that gap, and on \texttt{boxes} all of it, comes from the one frame of each segment in which the camera nearly stops before reversing and the contrast estimate fails. On the fastest real segment the gap reaches $4.7^\circ$/s. We do not claim parity, since an inertial measurement remains the better anchor, but the gap is small next to what the anchor buys. 

Nor does the network merely pass its anchor through. On the real sequences it improves on contrast maximization alone by $13$--$20\%$, by reducing the speed it estimates, which is too high there; on the synthetic sequences, where that estimate is nearly exact, the same reduction makes it worse. With the gyroscope as anchor, the network matches its own gyroscope against the motion-capture reference to within a few tenths of a degree per second, so the visual constraints are not simply overridden by the inertial signal.

We also characterised the residual error. It is almost entirely a magnitude error, at the step sizes we started from it grows with rotation speed ($r = +0.96$ over seven segments, against $+0.60$, not significant, at the adopted ones), and it is produced by the kinematic cost fitting $\wvec$ to a flow field that the visual side systematically underestimates: it appears only when that cost updates $\wvec$, and the ratio of its step size to the anchor weight controls it monotonically across the whole admissible range. One candidate explanation was tested and rejected (Sec.~\ref{sec:speed}): scene content, which two synthetic sequences sharing a trajectory but differing in scene leave unchanged. Why both synthetic sequences sit well above the trend fitted to the real ones remains open.

Separating the two quantities the model produces turned out to matter more than tuning either. A good angular velocity and a good picture are not obtained by the same choices, and on the accumulation window they are in direct opposition: from $10$ to $40$\,ms the angular-velocity error falls on every sequence while the mean $|r|$ of the reconstruction falls from $0.49$ to $0.12$ on the real ones (Sec.~\ref{sec:window_sweep}). The intensity update makes the point most sharply --- solving the Poisson equation exactly inside the loop instead of by one gradient step raises the low-frequency share of the intensity map on \texttt{poster} up to almost fourfold and changes $\wvec$ by less than $0.03^\circ$/s. Any configuration reported for this model should therefore say which of the two it was chosen for.

Four practical findings are worth recording. Lens distortion should be carried in the kinematic matrix rather than removed from the events, not for geometric reasons --- the two routes agree to $0.043$\,px --- but because rectifying event coordinates and re-binning them destroys about a fifth of the spatial support of $\Vmap$ at exactly the scale the gradient operates on. For \texttt{CMax-anchor}, twenty-five iterations per frame suffice for the angular velocity and the read-out, where the thesis uses $50$--$75$; the in-loop map still improves with more. The intensity map should be read out with the exact frequency-domain solution rather than taken from the iterative update, whose convergence rate per spatial frequency leaves the low frequencies unformed. And the tighter of the two contrast-maximization couplings, in which a gradient step on the contrast replaces the update rule for $\wvec$, is not a fusion of the two estimates, since the maps never act on its $\wvec$, and it is not robust: its step size scales with the square of the event count and it diverged outright on the densest sequence.

\subsection*{Outlook}

Several questions were left open for lack of time. The step sizes were swept only for \texttt{CMax-anchor}, and the anchor weight also for \texttt{thesis+IMU}; whether the networks without a reference also fail at other step sizes, and whether the order of the Cook updates matters, was not tested. Since no single $\kappa$ suits both kinds of data, choosing it per sequence, or online, is a further step.

Two explanations given above are untested: that the excursions grow through the flow constraint (Sec.~\ref{sec:regularisers}), and that the Fourier read-out wins because it suppresses the error of a non-integrable $\Gmap$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:reconstruction}). Nor do we know what raises $\beta$ on the synthetic sequences (Sec.~\ref{sec:maps_add}); a synthetic sequence that moves like the real ones would separate the effect of the motion from that of the ideal sensor. The curl term needs an independent validation, and an explanation of why it raises the curl inside the network on \texttt{poster}. A window that holds a fixed number of events rather than a fixed time might ease both the trade-off between the angular velocity and the image and the failure of the contrast estimate where the camera nearly stops.

\section{Inferred Maps for Every Model}
\label{app:maps}

Figure~\ref{fig:maps} shows the \texttt{CMax-anchor} model only.
Figures~\ref{fig:appendix_cook}--\ref{fig:appendix_cmax_v2} repeat the same
frames --- the last of each $1.5$\,s run of Table~\ref{tab:main} and frame
$43$ on \texttt{poster}, same segments, same parameters --- for the other four
models, so that every panel can be compared with its counterpart directly. Only
the event map is observed, and it is identical across the five figures.

\texttt{thesis+IMU} and \texttt{CMax-inloop} are close to
\texttt{CMax-anchor}: the real scenes are recognisable, the gradient
concentrates on their edges, and \texttt{bicycle} carries the same diagonal
ripple. The exception is \texttt{CMax-inloop} on \texttt{street}, which has
diverged ($400^\circ$/s and a $72^\circ$ axis error in Table~\ref{tab:main}):
no scene is left, and the flow is a nearly uniform field in a direction none of
the other models produces. The two unanchored models differ in two ways. On the synthetic
sequences \texttt{thesis} gives the cleanest reconstruction of any model,
without the ripple, which is the dissociation of
Sec.~\ref{sec:anchor_image}, whereas \texttt{cook} has diverged there to an
almost flat field ($\beta = 181$ and $325$ in Table~\ref{tab:main}). On the
real sequences both unanchored models produce recognisable images, but not always with the right polarity. When the true rotation turns away from their nearly constant estimate, $\Fmap$ and with it $\Gmap$ and $\Imap$ invert, which the constraints permit (a negative $\beta$ in~\eqref{eq:beta}). The \texttt{poster} and \texttt{dynamic} rows of Figs.~\ref{fig:appendix_cook} and~\ref{fig:appendix_thesis} are such frames: there the estimated and the true $\wvec$ point more than $90^\circ$ apart (cosine $-0.64$ to $-0.84$), and the correlation of the reconstruction with the APS is negative ($-0.26$ to $-0.76$). A signed mean over the frames would let such frames cancel the others, although every frame is an image, which is why the report scores $|r|$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:recon_metric}).


These are single frames, and as Sec.~\ref{sec:recon_experiments} notes the
reconstruction is not uniform in time: at the peaks of the rotation the
intensity map loses structure for a few tenths of a second before recovering.

\section{Convergence on the Synthetic Sequence}
\label{app:converge}

Figure~\ref{fig:converge_bicycle} is the counterpart of Fig.~\ref{fig:converge}
on the synthetic sequence. The convergence-rate pattern is the same --- the
sequential scheme has structure after one iteration, the simultaneous ones only
later (by about twenty-five on this sequence, against ten on \texttt{dynamic})
--- but here the sequential scheme does not stay stable: it diverges by
iteration ten, while the four simultaneous variants do not. Together the two
figures show that the stability difference is a property of the sequence,
whereas the difference in convergence rate holds on both.
