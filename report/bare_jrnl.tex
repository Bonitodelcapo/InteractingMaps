An Implementation and Evaluation of Two Formulations}

\author{Diana Leo, Gianluca Bonifazi% <-this % stops a space

\markboth{Event-based Machine Vision -- Project Report, TU Berlin}%
{Interacting Maps for Event Cameras}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Interacting Maps infers a scene interpretation from an event camera not by a
processing pipeline but by a set of retinotopic maps --- intensity, spatial
gradient, optical flow and angular velocity --- coupled by weak geometric
constraints and refined until mutually consistent. Two formulations exist, by
Cook \emph{et al.} and in Martel's dissertation, and neither, to our knowledge,
has a public implementation. We implement both on a shared camera model and
evaluation harness and evaluate them on five rotation sequences, three real and
two synthetic. As published, neither formulation tracks the angular velocity:
the scale ambiguity inherent to the constraints resolves itself arbitrarily,
leaving direction errors of $76$--$85^\circ$ on the real sequences, and on real
data the two update schemes fail in the same way. The method works only when an
absolute angular-velocity reference enters the cost minimised at every
iteration; supplying it once at initialisation is not sufficient. With a
gyroscope as that reference, the fused estimate matches the gyroscope's own
accuracy on the real sequences. We show that the reference need not be a second
sensor: a contrast-maximization estimate computed from the same events comes
within $1.5$--$2.6^\circ$/s of the gyroscope anchor on the three real
sequences. The residual error is almost entirely a magnitude error, governed by
the ratio of the two step sizes that act on the angular velocity, and lens
distortion belongs in the camera model rather than in the events.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{E}{vent} cameras are bio-inspired vision sensors whose pixels
operate independently and asynchronously: instead of sampling a full frame at a
fixed rate, every pixel emits an \emph{event} as soon as the logarithmic
brightness it observes has changed by more than a fixed contrast
threshold~\cite{lichtsteiner2008}. The resulting output is a sparse stream of
timestamped, signed brightness changes rather than a sequence of images. This
buys microsecond temporal resolution, a high dynamic range of about $120$~dB, low
power consumption and an absence of motion blur, which makes such sensors
attractive for high-speed and high-dynamic-range robotics~\cite{gallego2022survey}.
It also means that essentially none of the frame-based computer-vision toolbox
transfers directly: because a single event carries almost no information on its
own, algorithms have to be redesigned around the data they actually receive.

Most event-based methods answer this by keeping the familiar
\emph{processor-centric} structure of a pipeline: events are aggregated into some
intermediate representation, which is then transformed step by step into the
quantity of interest. A markedly different answer was given by Cook \emph{et al.}
with \emph{Interacting Maps}~\cite{cook2011}. There, the visual interpretation is
not computed by a chain of processing stages but is \emph{represented} by several
retinotopic maps --- light intensity $\Imap$, its spatial gradient $\Gmap$, the
optical flow $\Fmap$, and the camera's angular velocity $\wvec$ --- that are
recurrently coupled by a small number of constraints taken from geometry and from
the physics of image formation. The event data enters only through the temporal
intensity derivative map $\Vmap$; every other quantity is inferred. Each
constraint repeatedly nudges the maps it touches towards mutual consistency, and
the interpretation emerges from this relaxation rather than from a feed-forward
computation. Martel later generalised the idea in his
dissertation~\cite{martel2019thesis,martel2015relational} into a
\emph{quantity-centric} framework: a bipartite graph whose nodes are visual
quantities and whose factors are explicit energy functions, with inference
performed by message passing. The same rotational model is one of the instances
he derives, alongside variants that add temporal persistence, stereo, or fusion
with an inertial measurement unit (IMU).

Given only $\Vmap$, none of $\Imap$, $\Gmap$, $\Fmap$, $\wvec$ can be computed on
its own, and only jointly do the weak constraints determine a solution
(Sec.~\ref{sec:model}) --- the joint-estimation philosophy that later
event-based work reached from a different direction, e.g.\ the simultaneous
mosaicing and tracking of Kim \emph{et al.}~\cite{kim2014} or the
contrast-maximization framework of Gallego \emph{et al.}~\cite{gallego2018cmax}.

Despite being regularly cited as an early example of joint inference from events,
neither formulation appears to have a publicly available implementation, and to
our knowledge no independent evaluation of either has been published. Both
descriptions also leave decisions open that turn out to matter a great deal in
practice: the order in which the maps are updated, the relaxation step sizes,
how the maps are initialised, how lens distortion is accounted for, and --- most
importantly --- whether and how an absolute reference for the angular velocity is
supplied. Reproducing the method therefore means making these choices explicitly
and reporting what they cost.

This report describes such a re-implementation and its evaluation. Our
contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item An implementation of \emph{both} formulations sharing one data path,
      camera model and evaluation harness: Cook's sequential (Gauss--Seidel)
      relaxation and Martel's two-phase (Jacobi) message passing, which lets the
      two be compared on identical input.
\item An evaluation on the rotation sequences of the Event-Camera
      Dataset~\cite{mueggler2017} against motion-capture ground truth, and on two
      synthetic sequences of the ECRot dataset~\cite{guo2024cmaxslam}, reporting
      angular-velocity tracking error over time rather than single-frame results.
\item An empirical account of the scale ambiguity that both papers mention but
      neither quantifies (Sec.~\ref{sec:beta}): without an absolute reference the
      direction of $\wvec$ becomes uninformative, and the reference must enter
      the update at \emph{every} iteration --- once at initialisation is not
      enough.
\item A sensor-free replacement for that reference. Contrast
      maximization~\cite{gallego2018cmax} estimates $\wvec$ from the events with
      an objective that is \emph{not} invariant under the rescaling above, so it
      can take the gyroscope's place in the same cost without any other change to
      the model. We report two couplings: as a per-frame anchor, and as the
      in-loop update rule for $\wvec$.
\item Two ways of accounting for lens distortion --- undistorting the event
      coordinates before accumulation, or absorbing the distortion and its
      Jacobian into the kinematic matrix $\Cmat$ --- together with a
      network-free geometric check that the two describe the same lens. The
      second performed better and is used for the networks in all reported
      results.
\item A sensitivity analysis over the relaxation step sizes, the number of
      iterations per frame and the event-window duration.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the report is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background}
introduces the model and its two variants. Section~\ref{sec:implementation}
describes the implementation and how it was developed.
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports the experiments on the angular
velocity, Section~\ref{sec:recon_experiments} those on the reconstructed
intensity, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

(Sec.~\ref{sec:model}, Fig.~\ref{fig:model}), then the two inference schemes
(Secs.~\ref{sec:cook} and~\ref{sec:martel}), and finally the ambiguity that both
inherit and the estimator we use to remove it (Sec.~\ref{sec:scale}).

\subsection{Quantities and constraints}
\label{sec:model}

\subsubsection{Input}
An event camera pixel fires an event $e_k=(x_k,y_k,t_k,p_k)$ as soon as the
logarithmic intensity it observes has changed by a contrast threshold $\theta$
since the last event at that pixel~\cite{lichtsteiner2008}:
\begin{equation}
p_k = \begin{cases}
+1 & \text{if } \Delta \log \Imap(x_k,y_k,t_k) > \theta,\\
-1 & \text{if } \Delta \log \Imap(x_k,y_k,t_k) < -\theta .
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Events are accumulated over a short window of duration $\Delta t$ into an
\emph{event frame}, the per-pixel signed event count
\begin{equation}
E(x,y) \;=\; \sum_{t_k \in [t, t+\Delta t)} p_k \, \delta(x-x_k)\,\delta(y-y_k),
\label{eq:eventframe}
\end{equation}
which is a direct correlate of the temporal derivative of the (log) intensity.
Both formulations use a scalar map $\Vmap \approx \partial \Imap/\partial t$
derived from~\eqref{eq:eventframe} as the \emph{only} input to the network. In
what follows $\Imap$ always denotes log-intensity, as is standard for event
cameras.

\subsubsection{Quantities}
The world is modelled as a static scene observed by a camera that is fixed in
position but free to rotate. Besides $\Vmap$, the model carries the
log-intensity map $\Imap$, its spatial gradient $\Gmap$, the optical flow
$\Fmap$, and the camera's angular velocity $\wvec\in\mathbb{R}^3$ --- the only
non-retinotopic quantity in the network. A constant map $\Cmat$ encodes the
camera geometry.

\subsubsection{Constraints}
Three relations tie these quantities together. The first is the optical-flow
constraint equation~\cite{horn1981}, which follows from assuming that intensity
is only transported in the image and not created or destroyed,
$\mathrm{d}\Imap/\mathrm{d}t = 0$:
\begin{equation}
\Vmap_{x,y} + \Fmap_{x,y}\cdot\Gmap_{x,y} \;=\; 0 .
\label{eq:ofce}
\end{equation}
The second is the definition of the gradient, discretised with forward
differences,
\begin{equation}
\Gmap_{x,y} \;=\; \nabla \Imap_{x,y}
 \;=\; \big(\Imap_{x+1,y}-\Imap_{x,y},\; \Imap_{x,y+1}-\Imap_{x,y}\big)^{\!\top}.
\label{eq:spatial}
\end{equation}
The third states that the flow induced by a pure rotation is, at every pixel, a
linear function of $\wvec$,
\begin{equation}
\Fmap_{x,y} \;=\; \Cmat_{x,y}\,\wvec ,
\label{eq:kin}
\end{equation}
with the $2\times3$ kinematic matrix obtained by differentiating the projection
of a rotating 3-D point~\cite{martel2019thesis}
\begin{equation}
\Cmat_{x,y} =
\begin{pmatrix}
 x'y' & -(x'^2+1) & y' \\[2pt]
 y'^2+1 & -x'y' & -x'
\end{pmatrix},
\label{eq:cmat}
\end{equation}
where $(x',y')$ are the normalised image coordinates of pixel $(x,y)$; the
flow in pixels is this matrix premultiplied by $\operatorname{diag}(f_x,f_y)$,
as in~\eqref{eq:cdist}. Notably
$\Cmat_{x,y}$ does not depend on scene depth: rotational flow carries no
structure information, which is what makes the purely rotational model
self-contained.

\subsubsection{Underdetermination of the individual constraints}
Each constraint on its own is too weak to invert.
Equation~\eqref{eq:ofce} is one scalar equation in the four unknowns
$(\Fmap_{x,y},\Gmap_{x,y})$: given $\Vmap$ and $\Gmap$, the admissible $\Fmap$
form a line perpendicular to $\Gmap$ --- the aperture problem.
Equation~\eqref{eq:spatial} does not constrain $\Imap$ at all in the direction
of a constant offset, and $\Cmat_{x,y}$ in~\eqref{eq:kin} has rank~2 with kernel
spanned by $(x',y',1)^{\!\top}$, so a single flow vector leaves a whole line of
admissible $\wvec$. Only when all three are imposed simultaneously, over all
pixels, does a solution become determined. There is no feed-forward order in
which $\Imap$, $\Gmap$, $\Fmap$ and $\wvec$ can be computed from $\Vmap$; the
interpretation has to be found by iterating the constraints against each other.

\subsubsection{Scale ambiguity}
\label{sec:beta}
One degeneracy survives even the joint problem. For any $\beta\neq 0$ the
substitution
\begin{equation}
(\Imap,\Gmap,\Fmap,\wvec) \;\longmapsto\;
 (\beta\Imap,\;\beta\Gmap,\;\Fmap/\beta,\;\wvec/\beta)
\label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}
leaves \eqref{eq:ofce}--\eqref{eq:kin} satisfied identically. From a single
input frame it is therefore impossible to tell a slowly moving high-contrast
scene from a quickly moving low-contrast one, and since $\beta$ may be negative,
not even the sign of $\wvec$ is determined. Cook \emph{et al.} note that in
practice the ambiguity is resolved arbitrarily on the first frame by the random
initialisation and then held roughly constant by warm-starting each frame from
the previous converged state~\cite{cook2011}. As Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}
shows, this is exactly where the method is fragile in a tracking setting.

\subsection{Variant I: Sequential Relaxation (Cook \emph{et al.})}
\label{sec:cook}

Cook \emph{et al.}~\cite{cook2011} derive the updates from a single recipe: to
update a map from one relation, assume the other maps in that relation are
correct, compute the \emph{candidate} map --- the closest map to the current one
that would satisfy the relation --- and take a small step of size $\delta$
towards it. This is a relaxation method applied to local constraints rather than
to a boundary-constrained differential equation.

For the nonlinear relation~\eqref{eq:ofce} the candidate is not unique, so the
constraint is turned into the quadratic error
$Q_{\Vmap,\Gmap}(\Fmap_{x,y}) = (\Vmap_{x,y}+\Fmap_{x,y}\cdot\Gmap_{x,y})^2$ and
a gradient step is taken,
\begin{equation}
\Fmap_{x,y} \leftarrow \Fmap_{x,y}
 - 2\,\delta_{V\!F\!G}\,
   (\Vmap_{x,y}+\Fmap_{x,y}\cdot\Gmap_{x,y})\,\Gmap_{x,y},
\label{eq:cook_ofce}
\end{equation}
and symmetrically for $\Gmap$. For the linear relations the candidate is
explicit. From~\eqref{eq:spatial},
$\Gmap_{x,y}\leftarrow(1-\delta_{I\!G})\Gmap_{x,y}+\delta_{I\!G}\nabla
\Imap_{x,y}$; in the opposite direction a change of $\Imap_{x,y}$ also affects
the gradients at the neighbours $(x\!-\!1,y)$ and $(x,y\!-\!1)$, so the residual
$\Psi=\Gmap-\nabla\Imap$ is differenced back,
$\hat\Psi^{(x)}_{x,y}=\Psi^{(x)}_{x,y}-\Psi^{(x)}_{x-1,y}$ (and analogously in
$y$), giving
\begin{equation}
\Imap_{x,y} \leftarrow \Imap_{x,y}
  - \delta_{G\!I}\big(\hat\Psi^{(x)}_{x,y}+\hat\Psi^{(y)}_{x,y}\big),
\label{eq:cook_poisson}
\end{equation}
a discrete Poisson step. Finally $\Fmap$ is blended towards $\Cmat\wvec$, and
$\wvec$ is obtained by a linear least-squares fit over the whole flow field,
which aggregates every pixel's weak opinion into one estimate. Cook stores
$\Imap$ on a $(H\!+\!1)\times(W\!+\!1)$ grid so that forward differences yield a
full $H\times W$ gradient.

The updates are applied \textbf{sequentially}: each map is written immediately,
so later updates within the same sweep already see the new values. This is a
Gauss--Seidel scheme, and the result consequently depends on the order in which
the relations are visited --- an order the paper does not fix (ours is given in
Sec.~\ref{sec:networks}).

\subsection{Variant II: Energy-Based Message Passing (Martel)}
\label{sec:martel}

Martel~\cite{martel2019thesis,martel2015relational} recasts the same system in a
\emph{quantity-centric} framework. A model is a bipartite graph whose nodes are
visual quantities and whose factors are explicit energy functions, in the spirit
of a factor graph~\cite{kschischang2001}. For the rotational model the total energy is
the sum of the four costs
\begin{align}
\mathcal{C}_{\text{tot}} = \sum_{x,y} \Big[ &
  \|\nabla \Imap_{x,y}-\Gmap_{x,y}\|^2
+ (\Vmap_{x,y}+\Fmap_{x,y}\cdot\Gmap_{x,y})^2 \nonumber\\
&+ (\sigma(\Vmap_{x,y})-E_{x,y})^2
+ \|\Fmap_{x,y}-\Cmat_{x,y}\wvec\|^2 \Big],
\label{eq:energy}
\end{align}
where the third term makes the event map $E$ an explicit quantity of the model,
related to $\Vmap$ through the sensor's level-crossing nonlinearity $\sigma$
rather than being identified with it.

Inference minimises~\eqref{eq:energy} greedily by message passing
(Algorithm~6.5 of~\cite{martel2019thesis}) in two phases per iteration. In
\emph{Phase~1} every quantity sends its current value to the costs it
participates in, and each cost computes its partial derivative with respect to
each of its quantities. In \emph{Phase~2} the costs send those derivatives back
and every quantity updates. Because the gradient operator is linear, a quantity
touched by several costs simply sums the messages it receives; e.g.\ $\Gmap$
receives contributions from both the spatial and the flow-constraint cost.
Nonlinear relations use gradient steps as in~\eqref{eq:cook_ofce}, whereas linear
relations admit a closed-form optimum and are updated by blending towards it. For
the angular velocity, setting $\partial \mathcal{C}/\partial\wvec = 0$ yields the
normal equations $M\wvec^\star = v$ with
\begin{equation}
M=\sum_{x,y}\Cmat_{x,y}^{\!\top}\Cmat_{x,y}, \qquad
v=\sum_{x,y}\Cmat_{x,y}^{\!\top}\Fmap_{x,y},
\label{eq:normaleq}
\end{equation}
and the update $\wvec \leftarrow \wvec - \eta(\wvec - M^{-1}v)$. Since $M$
depends only on the pixel grid and the intrinsics, $M^{-1}$ is a $3\times3$
matrix that can be precomputed once.

The relation $\Gmap=\nabla\Imap$ is linear in the same sense, and the thesis
gives its closed-form optimum too. Recovering $\Imap$ from $\Gmap$ means solving
$\min_{\Imap}\lVert\nabla\Imap-\Gmap\rVert^2$, whose normal equations are the
Poisson equation $\Delta\Imap = \operatorname{div}\Gmap$. Because the Laplacian
is a convolution it is diagonal in the frequency domain, so the minimiser is one
division per spatial frequency $k$,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\Imap}(k) \;=\;
  \frac{\overline{D_x(k)}\,\widehat{\Gmap}_x(k)
      + \overline{D_y(k)}\,\widehat{\Gmap}_y(k)}
       {\lvert D_x(k)\rvert^2+\lvert D_y(k)\rvert^2},
\label{eq:poisson_exact}
\end{equation}
with $D$ the transform of the difference operator. The denominator vanishes at
$k=0$: the mean of $\Imap$ is the constant of integration and is not determined
by $\Gmap$, which is the same gauge freedom as the $\beta$-ambiguity of
Sec.~\ref{sec:scale}. Equation~\eqref{eq:cook_poisson} is one gradient step on
the same objective, and~\eqref{eq:poisson_exact} jumps to its minimum; the two
differ only in that the step of~\eqref{eq:cook_poisson} converges at a rate
$(1-\delta_{G\!I}\lvert k\rvert^2)$ per frequency, so the low frequencies move
far more slowly than the high ones. Sec.~\ref{sec:reconstruction} shows this is
what separates a recognisable intensity image from an edge map.

The decisive difference to Cook is that Phase~2 happens \textbf{simultaneously}:
all messages are computed from one common snapshot before anything is written.
This is a Jacobi scheme. It removes the dependence on update order and is the
property that makes the algorithm parallelisable and portable to
cellular-processor hardware, which is the thesis' motivation for it. The price
is paid in propagation speed (Sec.~\ref{sec:converge}), and Jacobi is also less
forgiving of an inconsistent starting state (Sec.~\ref{sec:networks}). The
thesis also derives extensions of the same
model --- a persistent world map, a stereo variant, and fusion with an inertial
measurement unit --- the last of which introduces a fourth cost pulling $\wvec$
towards an externally measured angular velocity. We implement this IMU fusion,
and then generalise it: the same cost accepts any external estimate of $\wvec$,
and we show in Sec.~\ref{sec:scale} that the gyroscope can be removed from it
altogether.

\subsection{Resolving the Scale Ambiguity}
\label{sec:scale}

Neither scheme removes the $\beta$-ambiguity of~\eqref{eq:beta}, because it is a
property of the constraints and not of the solver. Any remedy must therefore come
from outside them, in the form of a quantity that is stated in absolute units.
The thesis' own remedy is the IMU cost mentioned above: a gyroscope measures
$\wvec$ directly, so adding a term
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}_{\text{anchor}} \;=\; \|\wvec - \wvec_{\text{ext}}\|^2,
\qquad \wvec_{\text{ext}} = \wvec_{\text{gyro}}
\label{eq:anchorcost}
\end{equation}
pins down both scale and sign. We implement this first, and it works
(Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}). It is, however, an unsatisfying answer to a
question posed by a vision system: the quantity the network cannot determine is
supplied by a second sensor rather than recovered from the data.

The observation this report builds on is that $\wvec_{\text{ext}}$
in~\eqref{eq:anchorcost} need not come from a sensor at all. Any estimator whose
objective is \emph{not} invariant under the rescaling~\eqref{eq:beta} can occupy
the same place in the graph.

Contrast maximization~\cite{gallego2018cmax} offers the same anchor from the
events alone. Under a candidate angular velocity $\wvec$, each event is warped to
a common reference time $t_{\text{ref}}$ along the trajectory the hypothesis
implies,
\begin{equation}
\vec{p}_k' \;=\; \vec{p}_k + \big(\wvec\,(t_k-t_{\text{ref}})\big)\times\vec{p}_k ,
\label{eq:warp}
\end{equation}
with $\vec{p}_k$ the bearing vector of event $k$. The warped events are
accumulated into the image of warped events (IWE), and $\wvec$ is chosen to
maximise its contrast, here the variance
\begin{equation}
\wvec^\star = \arg\max_{\wvec}\ \operatorname{Var}\!\big[\mathrm{IWE}(\wvec)\big].
\label{eq:cmax}
\end{equation}
The IWE is sharpest at the true motion and blurs when the hypothesis is wrong in
\emph{either} direction or magnitude. Objective~\eqref{eq:cmax} is therefore
\emph{not} invariant under the rescaling~\eqref{eq:beta}, which is precisely the
property the three constraints lack. Setting
$\wvec_{\text{ext}} = \wvec^\star$ in~\eqref{eq:anchorcost} therefore turns the
IMU cost into a sensor-free one, without changing anything else in the model.
Two ways of coupling the estimator to the message passing are described in
Sec.~\ref{sec:coupling} and compared in Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}.


\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}

The system is implemented in Python with NumPy. All map updates are expressed as
whole-array operations, so a message-passing iteration costs a handful of vector
operations over the $180\times240$ sensor and no per-pixel loop is ever executed.

\subsection{Software Structure and Data Path}

The code is organised so that the two formulations share everything except the
solver: one module converts events into input frames, one builds the kinematic
matrix, one holds each network, one implements the contrast-maximization
estimator, and one drives the experiments. Any difference in output between the
two formulations is therefore attributable to the solver alone.

Events are read from the text format of~\cite{mueggler2017} and accumulated per
window into the signed-count image~\eqref{eq:eventframe}, which is then clipped
to $\pm c$ and divided by $c$ ($c = 10$), giving $\Vmap \in [-1,1]$. This is the standard
event-frame representation~\cite{gallego2022survey}. The accumulation window
$\Delta t$ serves simultaneously as the input window and as the time step
relating the network's internal $R$ (rad/frame) to the reported
$\wvec = R/\Delta t$ (rad/s).

Lens distortion can be removed at either end of this path, and both routes are
implemented. The first rectifies the event coordinates before accumulation and
leaves the kinematic matrix in its ideal pinhole form~\eqref{eq:cmat}. The second
keeps the events in their native pixel coordinates and moves the lens into the
geometry instead: $\Cmat_{x,y}$ is built from the true undistorted normalised
coordinates of each physical pixel and pre-multiplied by the analytic Jacobian
$J_D$ of the distortion map, so that the predicted flow is expressed in
distorted-pixel units,
\begin{equation}
\Cmat_{x,y} \;=\; \operatorname{diag}(f_x,f_y)\; J_D(x',y')\; A(x',y'),
\label{eq:cdist}
\end{equation}
with $A$ the matrix of~\eqref{eq:cmat}. The second route avoids re-quantising
rectified sub-pixel coordinates onto the pixel grid during accumulation, and the
networks use it in every result reported below; the contrast-maximization
estimator keeps the first route (Sec.~\ref{sec:coupling}).
Sec.~\ref{sec:distortion} verifies that the two routes describe the same lens
and explains why the second is preferred for the networks.

Evaluation segments are selected from the gyroscope record
(Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}); those of the real sequences span a factor of four in
rotation speed, which turns out to matter (Sec.~\ref{sec:speed}).

\subsection{The Two Networks}
\label{sec:networks}

Both networks are driven by the \emph{same} kinematic matrix --- the
distortion-aware form~\eqref{eq:cdist}, which reduces to~\eqref{eq:cmat}
without distortion --- and the same input frames, so that any difference in
their output is attributable to the update scheme alone rather than to the
camera model. The Cook network applies its updates in place, in the fixed order
$\wvec$ from $\Fmap$; $\Fmap$ and then $\Gmap$ from the flow
constraint~\eqref{eq:ofce}; $\Gmap$ from $\Imap$; $\Imap$ from $\Gmap$; and
$\Fmap$ from $\Cmat\wvec$, and stores $\Imap$ on the
$(H\!+\!1)\times(W\!+\!1)$ grid of~\cite{cook2011}. The Martel network is built
from \texttt{Quantity} and \texttt{Cost} objects that mirror
Algorithm~6.5 directly: each cost implements a single method that reads the
current values and adds its contribution to the gradient accumulator of every
quantity it touches, and the solver only alternates the two phases. Adding a
relation therefore means adding one class, which is how the anchoring cost and
the contrast-maximization cost were introduced; the solver's step function
needed only small additions (passing the anchor target and the events to the
costs that use them).

Two implementation decisions were necessary and are not stated in either source.
First, the simultaneous scheme requires a \emph{mutually consistent} initial
state: if $\wvec$ is initialised from a known value but $\Fmap$ is left at zero,
the kinematics cost sees a large residual and drives $\wvec$ to zero within a few
iterations, before the flow constraint has built any structure. Initialising
$\Fmap = \Cmat\wvec$ removes the problem entirely. Second, in the Martel network
the flow-constraint gradient in~\eqref{eq:cook_ofce} is clipped per pixel, since
it grows cubically when $|\Fmap|$ and $|\Gmap|$ are both large. Both networks
clip $\Imap$, $\Gmap$ and $\Fmap$ to fixed ranges after every iteration; the
Martel network also clips $\wvec$. Sec.~\ref{sec:speed} shows these bounds do
not shape the reported angular velocity.

\subsection{Coupling Contrast Maximization to the Network}
\label{sec:coupling}

The estimator of Sec.~\ref{sec:scale} is used with a fixed-time window equal to
one frame, a linear warp, signed polarity ($\pm1$ per event) in the IWE, the
variance objective, and a warm start from the previous frame's estimate; it
returns $\wvec$ in rad/s in the camera body frame, the same convention as the
network's $R = \wvec\Delta t$. In both couplings the estimator works on
rectified event coordinates with a pinhole warp. Because the IWE is formed by
bilinear voting of the float coordinates, no re-binning occurs and the sampling
artefact of Sec.~\ref{sec:distortion} does not arise; the network itself still
receives the raw events and the distortion-aware $\Cmat$. Two couplings to the
message passing were implemented. They
differ in \emph{where} the contrast optimisation happens relative to the
relaxation loop, and consequently in what determines $\wvec$. The first reuses
the anchoring cost~\eqref{eq:anchorcost} unchanged and only substitutes its
target, so it is the gyroscope-anchored model with one sensor swapped for an
estimator; the second replaces the update rule for $\wvec$ entirely.

\subsubsection{Per-frame anchor}
The first (\texttt{CMax-anchor}) treats contrast maximization as an external
measurement. Once per frame, before any relaxation, the estimator is run to
convergence on that frame's events, giving $\wvec_{\text{cmax}}$. The anchoring
cost of Sec.~\ref{sec:scale} then contributes
\begin{equation}
\Delta \wvec \;=\; \delta_{\text{anchor}}\,
  \big(\wvec - \wvec_{\text{cmax}}\big)
\label{eq:anchor}
\end{equation}
at \emph{every} one of the iterations of that frame, alongside all the other
costs. The kinematics cost continues to update $\wvec$ from the flow field as
in~\eqref{eq:normaleq}. The angular velocity is therefore determined jointly by
the anchor and by the network, and the two can disagree. Because the estimator is
called once per frame and its gradient is not needed, the optimisation uses a
derivative-free simplex search (Nelder--Mead) on the variance of the IWE
blurred by a Gaussian of $\sigma = 1$\,px, with the reference time at the
window midpoint.

\subsubsection{Interleaved update}
The second (\texttt{CMax-inloop}) removes the separation between the two
optimisations. The anchoring cost is dropped, and the kinematics cost is
restricted to propagating $\wvec$ into the flow field --- it still enforces
$\Fmap \leftarrow \Cmat\wvec$ but no longer updates $\wvec$ itself. In its place,
a single gradient-\emph{ascent} step on the contrast objective is taken once per
message-passing iteration on the network's $R = \wvec\Delta t$,
\begin{equation}
R \;\leftarrow\; R \;+\; \lambda\,
  \frac{\partial}{\partial R}\operatorname{Var}\!\big[\mathrm{IWE}(R/\Delta t)\big],
\label{eq:inloop}
\end{equation}
so that the contrast optimisation and the relaxation of the maps advance
together rather than one after the other. This requires the analytic gradient of
the objective, obtained by scattering the derivatives of the bilinear voting
kernel into three images and differentiating the variance of the unblurred IWE;
it was checked against finite differences during development. The reference
time is the midpoint between the first and last event of the window. In this
variant $\wvec$ is driven by contrast maximization \emph{alone}: the maps
receive it but never correct it.

The step size $\lambda$ in~\eqref{eq:inloop} is the one parameter without a
natural scale, since the gradient grows with the square of the number of events
in the window and therefore varies between sequences. We use a fixed
$\lambda = 10^{-4}$, equivalent to a step of $\lambda/\Delta t^2 = 0.25$ on
$\wvec$ (rad/s) at $\Delta t = 20$\,ms. It is stable on all but the densest
sequence, \texttt{street}, where it diverges (Table~\ref{tab:main}); an order of
magnitude larger diverges. Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments} shows that the difference
between the two couplings is almost entirely a difference in how the
\emph{magnitude} of $\wvec$ is determined, which is what one would expect from
the fact that only the first lets the kinematic constraint act on it.

\subsection{Deviations From the Published Descriptions}
\label{sec:deviations}

Neither source is complete enough to be implemented without choices. We list
ours explicitly, since some of them affect the comparison.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Shared camera model.} Cook expresses the flow relation as
  $\Fmap = m_{32}(\wvec\times\Cmat)$ with $\Cmat$ a map of unit ray directions.
  We use the kinematic matrix~\eqref{eq:cmat}, in its distortion-aware
  form~\eqref{eq:cdist}, for \emph{both} networks. The two
  express the same geometry, and sharing it isolates the update scheme as the
  only difference.
\item \textbf{Angular velocity from flow.} Cook minimises the point-to-line
  distance between $\wvec$ and the family of rotations compatible with each flow
  vector; we use the normal equations~\eqref{eq:normaleq} in both networks.
\item \textbf{The event cost is omitted.} The term $(\sigma(\Vmap)-E)^2$
  of~\eqref{eq:energy} is not implemented: $\Vmap$ is identified with the
  normalised event frame rather than inferred from $E$ through the sensor
  nonlinearity. $\Vmap$ is therefore treated as observed, as in~\cite{cook2011}.
\item \textbf{Intensity update.} Inside the message passing both networks use
  $\Imap \leftarrow \Imap - \eta\operatorname{div}(\Gmap-\nabla \Imap)$, i.e.\
  Cook's~\eqref{eq:cook_poisson}. For Cook's network this is exact: their
  Eq.~9 is a convex blend whose two $\Imap$ terms cancel. The thesis' Eq.~6.61
  carries an additional leading $\Imap$, a shrinkage term, which we therefore
  omit for the thesis network; Sec.~\ref{sec:regularisers} restores it and
  measures what it changes. The thesis' exact frequency-domain
  solution~\eqref{eq:poisson_exact} is also implemented, but as a read-out
  rather than as the in-loop update; Sec.~\ref{sec:reconstruction} measures both
  and explains why.
\item \textbf{Step sizes.} Neither source reports the values of the relaxation
  step sizes. Two of ours, $\delta_{F\!R}$ and $\delta_{\text{anchor}}$, were
  chosen by a parameter sweep and the other four set by hand
  (Table~\ref{tab:params}); Sec.~\ref{sec:sensitivity} examines all six.
\end{enumerate}

The data sources play strictly separate roles. The gyroscope (\texttt{imu.txt})
is a \emph{model input}: it supplies the anchor target of \texttt{thesis+IMU} at
every iteration, and the warm start $R_0 = \wvec_{\text{gyro}}\Delta t$ of the
first frame, which \emph{every} model shares so that all are compared from an
identical initial state. No model is therefore entirely gyroscope-free; the
contrast-anchored variants differ in not using it inside the loop. On the real
sequences the \emph{scoring reference} is the motion-capture record
(\texttt{groundtruth.txt}, an OptiTrack system at $200$\,Hz~\cite{mueggler2017}),
differenced between orientations in the body frame,
$\Delta R = R_1^{\!\top}R_2$; on the synthetic ones it is the simulator's exact
angular velocity. Because the reference and the gyroscope come from different
instruments, scoring the gyroscope-anchored model this way is not circular;
comparing it against the gyroscope itself would be, and we never do.

The width of the differencing bracket matters more than it appears. Over a
single 20\,ms window the reference is dominated by pose noise: the gyroscope
scores $8$--$25^\circ$/s against it depending on the segment, so the metric
cannot resolve anything better than that regardless of the model. All results
below use a $\pm50$\,ms bracket, and every table reports the gyroscope's own
error against the same reference as an explicit noise floor.

Each frame is scored by the error $\|\wvec_{\text{est}}-\wvec_{\text{ref}}\|$ in
$^\circ$/s, the angle between the two vectors, and the scale factor
$\beta = \|\wvec_{\text{ref}}\| / \|\wvec_{\text{est}}\|$. The last is reported
separately throughout, because Sec.~\ref{sec:beta} predicts that the direction
and the magnitude of $\wvec$ fail independently, and they do.

Finally, $\wvec$ is \emph{recurrent}. It is initialised once before the first
frame and is never overwritten afterwards; each frame starts from the previous
frame's converged state, as in~\cite{cook2011}. No per-frame ground-truth
re-anchoring is used anywhere, so drift over a segment is fully visible.

\subsection{Development and Validation}

\subsubsection{Working with a language model}
The repository was written with the assistance of a large language model. We
did not ask for an implementation of a method by name, since the model has no
reliable knowledge of either source; instead we supplied the source material
--- the two papers, the relevant dissertation chapter and the dataset's file
format --- and asked for a skeleton: the module layout, the event-loading path,
and class stubs for the two networks with the update rules transcribed from the
numbered equations. That skeleton was a reasonable starting point, and it is
where most of the time was saved.

It was not, however, correct. The generated code was structurally sound but
silently substituted plausible variants for what the text says --- a centred
difference where the paper specifies a forward one, a symmetric update where the
paper's is asymmetric, a normalisation the paper does not have --- which produce
slightly wrong answers rather than errors. Every generated update rule was
therefore checked line by line against the equation it claimed to implement,
and anything not traceable to a numbered equation was treated as our own design
decision and documented (Sec.~\ref{sec:deviations}). As a reader, explaining a
derivation step or what a piece of our code computed, the model caught several
of our own errors; whether a result was right it could not tell us, which is
what the validation below is for.

\subsubsection{Layered validation}
The system was built in layers, each validated before the next was added: the
Cook network, then the Martel network, then the anchoring cost, then distortion
handling, then contrast maximization. Because the network's output is a single
3-vector that is hard to attribute to any one component, we relied on checks that
isolate components rather than on end-to-end accuracy alone. The distortion
geometry was verified against \texttt{cv2.projectPoints} and by a round-trip
test, and the two distortion routes were shown to agree on a fixed-rotation warp
to sub-pixel accuracy with the network removed
(Sec.~\ref{sec:distortion}). The analytic gradient of the contrast objective was
checked against finite differences. The contrast-maximization front-end was
validated against \emph{both} the gyroscope and the motion-capture reference
before being connected to
the network at all, so that a later failure could be attributed to the coupling
rather than to the estimator.

One methodological lesson is worth recording, because it shaped the protocol of
Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}: conclusions drawn from a single short segment did not
survive. A configuration that appeared clearly best on a few frames of one
medium-speed segment behaved qualitatively differently on a faster one. All
results below are therefore reported over multiple segments spanning a range of
rotation speeds, and never as a single aggregate number.


\section{Experiments on Angular Velocity}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:protocol}

\subsubsection{Sequences and reference}

We evaluate on three rotation sequences of the Event-Camera
Dataset~\cite{mueggler2017}, recorded with a DAVIS240C ($240\times180$) that
provides events, a gyroscope and OptiTrack motion-capture poses simultaneously,
and on two synthetic sequences from ECRot~\cite{guo2024cmaxslam}, rendered with
a model of the same sensor. Table~\ref{tab:datasets} lists them.

The synthetic camera is an \emph{idealised} DAVIS240C, and the difference is
worth stating because it bears on Sec.~\ref{sec:distortion}. It shares the
resolution and, to within half a percent, the focal length of the real sensor
($f = 200$ against $199.1$/$198.8$\,px), but its principal point sits at the
exact image centre where the real one is offset by $(12.2, 20.7)$\,px, and it
carries no lens distortion at all against $k_1 = -0.37$ for the real camera.
Each sequence is processed with its own calibration throughout, so the
distortion-aware kinematic matrix~\eqref{eq:cdist} reduces to the ideal pinhole
form on the synthetic data.

The synthetic sequences are not a second kind of motion: their rotation is
spread across all three axes much as the real ones are ($30\%$, $46\%$ and
$24\%$ of $\lVert\wvec\rVert$ on $x$, $y$ and $z$). What they isolate is the
\emph{sensor}. With no lens distortion and no sensor noise they are the
best case the method can be given, and Sec.~\ref{sec:baseline} uses them as
exactly that: a measurement of what the front-end achieves when nothing in the
imaging is working against it.

We excluded \texttt{shapes\_rotation} a priori, without running it: its scene
contains a few isolated shapes on a blank background, whereas both formulations
assume a scene that produces events densely across the image, so that the flow
constraint has a spatial gradient to act on almost everywhere.

\texttt{dynamic\_rotation} is included with a caveat. Part of its scene --- a
person --- can move independently of the camera, which the model of
Sec.~\ref{sec:model} cannot represent: it explains all image motion as
ego-rotation. Its segment is therefore chosen by a different criterion from the
other sequences: not by rotation speed, which would select a stretch in which
the person stands up and walks across the field of view, but as the stretch in
which the scene is most nearly static --- the person seated, the camera
providing essentially all of the image motion. We report the sequence because
the reconstructed maps remain qualitatively correct on it
(Sec.~\ref{sec:recon_experiments}), and read its angular-velocity numbers as an
indication of robustness rather than as a clean measurement.

The synthetic sequences depict busy scenes --- a street with pedestrians, a
cyclist --- but are nonetheless static in the sense the model requires: they are
rendered by rotating a virtual camera inside a fixed high-resolution panorama,
so the depicted people are frozen and all image motion is ego-motion. We
verified this on the sequences themselves: at the closest revisit of a camera
orientation, $5$\,s apart, the two frames differ only by a uniform,
edge-aligned residual consistent with the residual $1.8^\circ$ of
misalignment, with no localised difference anywhere in the scene. The contrast
with \texttt{dynamic\_rotation} is therefore one of scene \emph{motion}, not
scene content.

The two synthetic sequences earn their place on grounds of measurement precision
rather than realism. Their bags carry the simulator's own angular velocity, so
the reference is exact and needs no differencing: their simulated gyroscope
agrees with it to about $0.1^\circ$/s instantaneously and to $0.68^\circ$/s
under the $\pm50$\,ms averaging used for scoring (Sec.~\ref{sec:harness}),
against $6$--$9^\circ$/s for the real sequences. They can therefore resolve
differences between models that the real data cannot, at the cost of lacking
both real sensor noise and a real lens.
Usefully, the two were generated along the \emph{same} camera trajectory --- we
verified that their pose records agree to $5\times10^{-7}$ in quaternion
components --- over different scenes, \texttt{street} being the denser in
events. They therefore form a
controlled pair, varying scene content with the motion held fixed, which
Sec.~\ref{sec:speed} uses to rule out one explanation of the magnitude error.

Each segment spans $150$ frames of $\Delta t = 20$\,ms, i.e.\ three seconds of
continuous tracking --- long enough for drift to appear, which shorter
evaluations hide. The scale-ambiguity analysis (Table~\ref{tab:anchor},
Fig.~\ref{fig:beta_speed}) uses all $150$; the main comparison
(Table~\ref{tab:main}) and the figures drawn from its runs use the first $75$
($1.5$\,s). The segment reported per sequence is the one of median
rotation speed, chosen on that criterion alone and not on any model's error.
Because Sec.~\ref{sec:speed} finds that accuracy depends systematically on
rotation speed, reporting a single segment per sequence would otherwise amount
to an undeclared choice of operating point; Fig.~\ref{fig:beta_speed} therefore
shows all seven segments we measured on \texttt{boxes} and \texttt{poster}.

Segment start times come from a pass over the gyroscope record that flags
windows of $0.5$--$3$\,s with low angular-velocity variance (relative standard
deviation of $\|\wvec\|$ below $0.25$; on \texttt{boxes} the flagged windows
are $0.5$\,s long), and the tracks run $1.5$ or $3$\,s from those starts. Over
the tracks themselves
$\wvec$ is not constant --- on \texttt{poster} it goes through a full reversal of
$\omega_x$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:tracking}) --- and it need not be: the constraints of
Sec.~\ref{sec:model} hold instantaneously for any $\wvec$, and the differenced
reference only requires $\wvec$ to be roughly constant across its $\pm50$\,ms
bracket. A reference obtained by fitting a rotation spline through the poses
and differentiating it analytically would remove even that requirement; we did
not implement one.

\subsubsection{Lens distortion}
\label{sec:distortion}

The DAVIS240C lens is appreciably distorted ($k_1 = -0.37$), and
Sec.~\ref{sec:implementation} described two ways of accounting for it. Both are
correct as geometry; they differ in where the correction is applied, and that
turns out to matter for a reason unrelated to the geometry.

We first verified that the distortion-aware kinematic
matrix~\eqref{eq:cdist}, which is our own derivation rather than something taken
from either source, encodes the lens correctly. Because the network's scale
ambiguity would confound any end-to-end comparison, the check is made with the
network removed, using contrast maximization as an independent estimator: one
path undistorts the events and warps them under a pinhole model, the other keeps
the events raw and carries the lens through the warp, as $\Cmat$ does. Three
checks agree. The forward distortion model matches \texttt{cv2.projectPoints} to
$2.8\times10^{-14}$\,px; undistortion and distortion compose to the identity to
$10^{-8}$\,px; and --- the decisive one --- at a fixed rotation, warping the
distortion-aware way and then undistorting the result lands on the pinhole warp
to $0.043$\,px, well below one pixel. The two routes are the same map.

The difference is one of sampling, not geometry, and it is visible directly in
the input. \texttt{cv2.undistortPoints} returns sub-pixel float coordinates,
which the accumulation of~\eqref{eq:eventframe} must then bin onto the integer
pixel grid. The lens is barrel-distorted, so rectification stretches the
periphery: neighbouring sensor pixels land up to $1.5$\,px apart, binning skips
the pixels in between, and because the mapping is fixed the same pixels stay
empty in every frame. The result is a concentric moir\'e pattern that is not
present in the data (Fig.~\ref{fig:distortion}), and the outer ring of the
sensor falls outside the frame altogether. Over the same events, undistorting
first reduces the number of occupied pixels from $43.2$k to $33.7$k: roughly a
fifth of the spatial support of $\Vmap$ is lost. The spatial gradient $\Gmap$ is
computed by differencing neighbouring pixels of $\Imap$, so an artefact at
exactly that scale is the worst kind for this model.

Carrying the lens in $\Cmat$ avoids the resampling entirely: the events stay on
the pixels that produced them, and the distortion is applied to the
\emph{prediction} instead, where it costs nothing. We therefore use the
distortion-aware $\Cmat$ in both networks for every result in this report; the
contrast-maximization anchor, which never bins its coordinates, undistorts its
events first (Sec.~\ref{sec:coupling}).

Unless stated otherwise all runs use the parameters of

\subsection{Main Results}
\label{sec:mainresults}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports every model on the five sequences at the
configuration of Table~\ref{tab:params}. How $\delta_{F\!R}$ and
$\delta_{\text{anchor}}$ were chosen, and why no single value is best for
both real and synthetic data, is given in Sec.~\ref{sec:sensitivity}.

Without an absolute reference, neither formulation tracks
(Sec.~\ref{sec:beta_exp}): on the real sequences direction errors of
$76$--$85^\circ$ leave the recovered axis uninformative, and total errors of
$43$--$57^\circ$/s are as large as the rotation being measured.

Adding an anchor changes the picture entirely, and most of what the anchor
provides does not depend on where it comes from. Both anchored models recover the
rotation axis to $6$--$13^\circ$ on the real sequences and to $1$--$2^\circ$ on
the synthetic ones, against $76$--$85^\circ$ without an anchor, and both bring
$\beta$ to within $14\%$ of unity where the unanchored \texttt{cook} reaches
$181$ and $338$ on the two synthetic sequences. Measured against what the anchor
is for, the two are interchangeable.

Measured on total error they are not quite. The gyroscope is consistently ahead
of the contrast estimate, by $1.4^\circ$/s on \texttt{boxes}, $2.4$ on
\texttt{poster}, $2.5$ on \texttt{dynamic} and $0.8$ on both synthetic
sequences. The gap is real and we do
not claim parity: an inertial measurement of $\wvec$ remains a better anchor than
an estimate inferred from the events. What the comparison does establish is that
the estimate is close enough to stand in for it --- within $1$--$3^\circ$/s of a
dedicated sensor, on a quantity the network cannot determine at all on its own,
using nothing but the data already being processed.

On the real sequences \texttt{cook} and \texttt{thesis} differ by a few
percent and fail in the same way; on the synthetic pair the sequential scheme
settles at a far more extreme scale ($\beta = 181$ and $338$ against
$2.6$ and $2.5$), consistent with its instability in Sec.~\ref{sec:converge}.

Figure~\ref{fig:tracking} shows what the aggregate numbers describe. The
anchored network follows the reference through a full reversal of $\omega_x$ and
the onset of $\omega_z$, with a residual that is visible but bounded. The
unanchored network is the flat trace: having settled on one scale in the first
few frames, it stays there, reproducing neither the reversal nor the magnitude.
It is not tracking poorly --- it is not tracking.

Because the synthetic reference is exact (Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}), the

$0.02$ in $\beta$ for \texttt{thesis+IMU} and \texttt{CMax-anchor}, despite

magnitude too small, while still producing a self-consistent set of maps. That
the same model reaches two such different scales on two sequences filmed along
the \emph{same} trajectory is the $\beta$-ambiguity in its purest form.

\subsection{Convergence Within a Frame}
\label{sec:converge}

Everything above measures the state the network reaches after $75$ iterations.
How it gets there is worth showing, because it is the mechanism the two update
schemes actually differ in. Figure~\ref{fig:converge} follows the intensity map
through the iterations of a single frame, with $\Imap$ initialised from noise at
that frame rather than carried over from the previous one ($\Fmap$ and $\wvec$
start from the gyroscope reading at the beginning of the segment). $\Imap$ is
never observed; nevertheless the scene assembles itself over a few tens of
iterations, sharpening monotonically to iteration $75$, by which point the desk,
the monitor and the objects on the desk are legible. Nothing in the model
computes $\Imap$ from $\Vmap$ --- it emerges from the three constraints being
applied against each other.

The two update schemes reach that state very differently. The sequential scheme
of Cook \emph{et al.} has recognisable structure after a \emph{single}
iteration, because each sub-update immediately sees the output of the one
before it, so information crosses the whole network within one sweep. The
simultaneous scheme is still noise at iterations one and three and only develops
structure by about ten, because every message is computed from a common snapshot
and therefore travels one relation per iteration. By iteration $75$ the two are
comparable, which is why they score alike on the real sequences in
Table~\ref{tab:main}: the difference between Gauss--Seidel and Jacobi in this
model is one of convergence rate, not of the solution reached.

Speed is not free. On the synthetic sequence the sequential scheme is also the
less stable of the two: it produces a good map at the first iterations and then
diverges, collapsing to an almost flat field by iteration ten, while all four
simultaneous variants converge normally on the same input
(Appendix~\ref{app:converge}). This is consistent with the thesis' preference
for the simultaneous scheme on grounds other than accuracy.

\subsubsection{Effect of the anchor}
\label{sec:beta_exp}

The three constraints of Sec.~\ref{sec:model} determine $\wvec$ only up to the
rescaling~\eqref{eq:beta}. Table~\ref{tab:anchor} shows what that costs on real
data. Without an absolute reference, neither formulation produces a usable
estimate over three seconds: direction errors of $72$--$100^\circ$ mean the
recovered axis carries essentially no information about the true one, and the
magnitude is worse still --- on the fastest segment the Cook network settles at
$\beta \approx 148$, i.e.\ an angular velocity too small by two orders of
magnitude. This is not a tuning failure. It is the ambiguity resolving itself
arbitrarily, exactly as~\eqref{eq:beta} permits, and then being carried forward
by the recurrent state.

It is worth being precise about what does \emph{not} fix this. Every model in
Table~\ref{tab:anchor}, including the two pure-vision ones, is warm-started from
the gyroscope: $\wvec$ begins at the correct value on frame~0. That is
sufficient only for the first few frames. Once the relaxation is free to rescale
$\Gmap$ against $\Fmap$, nothing in the cost function prefers the initial scale
to any other, and the estimate leaves it. An absolute reference supplied
\emph{once} therefore does not anchor the model; it has to be present in the
cost that is minimised at every iteration.

Adding the gyroscope term~\eqref{eq:anchorcost} does exactly that, and the
failure disappears: the error falls by a factor of six to nine, direction error
from $72$--$100^\circ$ to $12$--$15^\circ$, and on the slowest segment $\beta$
returns to $1.00$. On that segment the anchored model scores $5.44^\circ$/s
against the motion-capture reference while the gyroscope that anchors it scores
$5.51^\circ$/s. The same ordering appears on all three real sequences of
Table~\ref{tab:main} ($5.8$ against $6.2$, $8.7$ against $8.8$,
$7.0$ against $7.2^\circ$/s). Since the reference is a third instrument,
independent of both, this is unlikely to be an artefact of the comparison. The
margins, however, are $0.1$--$0.4^\circ$/s, far below the $6$--$9^\circ$/s
floor of the reference, and on the fastest segment of Table~\ref{tab:anchor}
the order reverses ($11.8$ against $11.6^\circ$/s). What the data support is
that on real data the fused estimate matches its gyroscope to within a few
tenths of a degree per second --- consistent with the visual constraints
filtering the inertial signal rather than being overridden by it, but not a
demonstration that they improve on it.

\subsubsection{Contrast maximization as the anchor}
\label{sec:cmax_exp}

The anchor of~\eqref{eq:anchorcost} does not have to be a measurement. Replacing
$\wvec_{\text{gyro}}$ by the contrast-maximization estimate leaves the model
otherwise untouched and removes the second sensor from the loop.

Compared at equal anchor weight, the substitution costs something, and it is
worth being exact about how much. On the real segments of
Tables~\ref{tab:main} and~\ref{tab:anchor}, the gyroscope-anchored model is
ahead of the contrast-anchored one by between $1.5$ and $4.7^\circ$/s, the gap
widest on the fastest segment ($16.5$ against $11.8^\circ$/s at
$1.67$\,rad/s). The synthetic sequences cannot inform this comparison, since
their simulated gyroscope is nearly exact (Sec.~\ref{sec:baseline}). Set against $107^\circ$/s for the same network
unanchored, both recover the overwhelming majority of what an anchor provides;
between the two, an inertial measurement remains the better reference.

The comparison is only meaningful because both anchors carry the same weight,
and it is sensitive to that choice: at a weaker $\delta_{\text{anchor}}$ the two
move closer together, which would flatter the visual anchor rather than inform
about it. We therefore report both at the same value throughout, and note from
Table~\ref{tab:sweep} that the ordering holds at every weight tested except
the weakest on \texttt{poster}.

\subsubsection{Magnitude determination in the two couplings}
\texttt{CMax-anchor} has the lower direction error on every segment of
Table~\ref{tab:anchor} ($19.3$, $12.3$, $13.5^\circ$ against $33.9$, $16.6$,
$14.3^\circ$), and at the parameters of Table~\ref{tab:params} the lower total
error as well. At the original $\delta_{F\!R} = 0.5$,
$\delta_{\text{anchor}} = 0.3$ the comparison of total error was less
straightforward, and it illustrates why $\beta$ is reported separately: on the
fast segment \texttt{CMax-inloop} had the \emph{lower} total error ($24.0$ vs
$32.8^\circ$/s) despite nearly twice the direction error, because the two
estimators erred in opposite directions in magnitude --- $\beta = 0.77$
against $1.18$ --- and for \texttt{CMax-inloop} the magnitude and direction
errors partially cancel inside the norm. Ranking on total error alone would
have selected the worse estimator. The
difference between the couplings is therefore almost entirely a difference in
how $\|\wvec\|$ is determined, which is what Sec.~\ref{sec:coupling} predicts:
only \texttt{CMax-anchor} leaves the kinematic constraint acting on $\wvec$.

\subsubsection{Speed dependence of the magnitude error}
\label{sec:speed}
Pooling the seven segments measured on \texttt{boxes} and \texttt{poster},
which span $\|\wvec\| = 0.42$ to $1.67$\,rad/s, makes the pattern unambiguous. At the
original $\delta_{F\!R} = 0.5$, $\delta_{\text{anchor}} = 0.3$ the scale factor
of \texttt{CMax-anchor} is almost perfectly correlated with rotation speed,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{corr}\big(\|\wvec\|,\ \beta\big) = +0.96 \quad (n=7,\ p<0.001),
\label{eq:betacorr}
\end{equation}
rising monotonically from $\beta = 0.80$ at the slowest segment to
$\beta = 1.18$ at the fastest --- from a $25\%$ overestimate to a $15\%$
underestimate of $\|\wvec\|$. For \texttt{CMax-inloop} the same correlation is $-0.47$, which at
this sample size is not significant ($p \approx 0.3$) and is in any case of the
opposite sign: whatever governs its magnitude, it is not the same
speed-dependent shrinkage.

That contrast localises the mechanism. The two variants share the estimator, the
input, the camera model and the maps, and differ in exactly one respect: whether
the kinematic cost is allowed to update $\wvec$. The shrinkage appears only when
it is, so it is produced by the kinematic update fitting $\wvec$ to a flow field
that is systematically too slow. The gyroscope-anchored model shows the same
trend at that configuration ($\beta = 1.00, 1.16, 1.33$ over the three poster
segments, slowest to fastest), confirming that the effect belongs to the network and
not to any particular anchor.

Why the inferred flow is too small follows from the ambiguity itself. Equation
\eqref{eq:beta} leaves the split of the flow constraint between $\Fmap$ and
$\Gmap$ undetermined, and the relaxation resolves it from the data. The anchor
constrains $\wvec$ but nothing constrains that split, so the kinematic cost ---
which at the original configuration carried a larger step size than the anchor,
$0.5$ against $0.3$ --- pulls $\wvec$ towards whatever $\Fmap$ the visual
side has settled on, and that flow field is too slow.

One natural refinement of this account is that the split should depend on scene
contrast: a high-contrast scene drives $\Gmap$ up, so a correspondingly smaller
$\Fmap$ satisfies $\Vmap = -\Fmap\cdot\Gmap$ equally well. The two synthetic
sequences test exactly this and refute it. They were rendered along the
\emph{same} camera trajectory over different scenes, \texttt{street} the
denser in events, yet at matched parameters they yield
$\beta = 1.485$ and $1.482$ over the same three seconds as the real segments
--- indistinguishable. Scene content, at least as
event density varies it, does not set the magnitude error.

That leaves a difference we cannot presently explain. At
$\|\wvec\| = 1.32$\,rad/s both synthetic sequences give $\beta \approx 1.48$
where the trend of~\eqref{eq:betacorr} fitted to the real ones predicts
$\approx 1.10$. Whatever distinguishes them is shared by both synthetic
sequences and is not their scene, their event density or their rotation speed.
We report the discrepancy rather than resolve it.

We can rule out one candidate explanation. The value clipping of
Sec.~\ref{sec:implementation} is not responsible, although it is active. At the
parameters of Table~\ref{tab:params} only the gradient map ever reaches its
bound, and only on \texttt{poster}: in $25$ of its $75$ frames, on $0.6\%$ of
the pixels on average and at most $7.8\%$; $\Imap$ and $\Fmap$ never do. It is
nonetheless not the cause, because widening all three bounds by a factor of
twenty changes the error by less than $0.05^\circ$/s and $\beta$ by less than
$0.002$ on every sequence. The saturation is
therefore a consequence of the same imbalance, not its origin, and the effect is
a property of the constraint system rather than of the safeguards around it.

The account above makes a testable prediction: if the shrinkage is produced by
the kinematic update out-weighting the anchor, then reducing $\delta_{F\!R}$
should weaken the dependence on speed, not merely shift it. Repeating the
seven-segment measurement at $\delta_{F\!R} = 0.1$ confirms this. The
correlation of~\eqref{eq:betacorr} falls from $+0.96$ to $+0.60$ --- no longer
significant at $n = 7$ --- and the spread of $\beta$ across the full range of
rotation speeds halves, from $0.80$--$1.18$ to $0.79$--$0.97$. The residual
$\beta < 1$ is a roughly constant offset rather than a trend. A single step size
in a cost the constraint system leaves undetermined therefore governs the entire
speed dependence, which is the strongest evidence we have that the mechanism is
the one proposed here.

Strictly, it is the balance between the two step sizes that matters, not
$\delta_{F\!R}$ alone. Both act on $\wvec$ in the same update,
$\wvec \leftarrow \wvec - \delta_{F\!R}(\wvec-\wvec^\star)
 - \delta_{\text{anchor}}(\wvec-\wvec_{\text{ext}})$, whose fixed point depends
only on their ratio. The $1.5$\,s grid of Sec.~\ref{sec:objective} confirms it:
settings with equal ratio give the same mean error to within $0.07^\circ$/s,
e.g.\ $(\delta_{F\!R}, \delta_{\text{anchor}}) = (0.1, 0.3)$ and $(0.3, 0.9)$
both $8.64^\circ$/s on the real sequences and $10.43$ on the synthetic ones.
Lowering $\delta_{F\!R}$ from $0.5$ to $0.1$ while raising the anchor from
$0.3$ to $0.5$ divides the ratio by eight.

A control supports the same conclusion. \texttt{CMax-inloop}, in which the
kinematic cost does not act on $\wvec$ at all, returns \emph{identical} results
at both configurations on every sequence for which both were run
(the grey points of Fig.~\ref{fig:beta_speed} are the same at both): neither $\delta_{F\!R}$ nor $\delta_{\text{anchor}}$
reaches it. The parameters act only through the paths the model says they do.

The two couplings therefore trade off along different axes.
\texttt{CMax-anchor} has the better direction estimate on every segment of both
sequences, and its magnitude degrades with speed. \texttt{CMax-inloop} keeps a
speed-independent magnitude and loses on direction. At the original
$\delta_{F\!R}$ the speed dependence was strong enough to invert the ranking on
total error on the fastest segment; at the adopted $\delta_{F\!R} = 0.1$, which
weakens it, \texttt{CMax-anchor} has the lower total error on all seven
segments.

This reframes the fast-segment result. It is not a failure of contrast
maximization, which supplies an accurate magnitude there, but a limitation of
the relaxation that no anchor can repair.

\subsection{Sensitivity and Ablation}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

\subsubsection{Procedure}
\label{sec:objective}
The model produces two things we care about --- an angular velocity and an
intensity map --- and the parameters of Table~\ref{tab:params} were set by the
angular-velocity error alone; no reconstruction measurement entered that
choice. The asymmetry is deliberate: $\wvec$ has an independent reference in
the motion-capture poses and the simulator twist, whereas $\Imap$ has none,
since the APS frames differ from log-intensity in exposure and in time and can
support a comparison between reconstructions but not an absolute one. The
configuration reported here is therefore not the best one for image quality.
Reconstruction quality is reported throughout, as the column $|r|$ of
Table~\ref{tab:main} and in Sec.~\ref{sec:recon_experiments}, but as a measured
property of a configuration chosen for something else; the configurations that
do maximise it are given in Sec.~\ref{sec:image_settings}, and no
angular-velocity result in this report uses them.

The analysis has two stages, both at $1.5$\,s on all five sequences. First,
$\delta_{F\!R}$ and $\delta_{\text{anchor}}$ were chosen together on a
$5\times5$ grid, $\delta_{F\!R} \in \{0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5\}$ against
$\delta_{\text{anchor}} \in \{0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9\}$, with
\texttt{CMax-anchor}; the other four rates had been set by hand early on and
were held fixed. Relative to the original values the adopted pair lowers the
mean error on both kinds of data --- from $9.5$ to $9.3^\circ$/s on the real
sequences and from $25.9$ to $7.2^\circ$/s on the synthetic ones --- but not on
every sequence: on \texttt{dynamic} it rises from $8.7$ to $9.6^\circ$/s. It is
a compromise, not the optimum of either kind of data: on the same grid the real
sequences are best at $\delta_{F\!R} = 0.3$, $\delta_{\text{anchor}} = 0.5$
($8.2^\circ$/s) and the synthetic ones at $\delta_{F\!R} = 0.02$,
$\delta_{\text{anchor}} = 0.9$ ($2.4^\circ$/s). Second, every parameter of
Table~\ref{tab:params} --- the six rates, the iteration count and the window
--- was varied one at a time around the adopted configuration, the anchor
weight for both anchors (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}). The configuration is thus
tuned in two dimensions only, and the second stage measures what each of the
other parameters changes around it.

Two effects hold on all five sequences independently: removing the anchor
degrades every one, and $\beta$ rises monotonically with $\delta_{F\!R}$ and
with $\delta_{V\!F\!G}$ on every one. This is what makes them worth reporting,
since the sequences differ in scene, in sensor (real against simulated) and in
rotation speed by a factor of $1.8$. Where the error is lowest, by contrast,
differs between real and synthetic data on every rate that matters
(Sec.~\ref{sec:realvssynth}).

\subsubsection{Anchor ablation}
Setting $\delta_{\text{anchor}} = 0$ makes the anchoring
cost~\eqref{eq:anchorcost} contribute nothing, leaving the network exactly as
Cook and Martel specify it; the row reproduces \texttt{thesis} of
Table~\ref{tab:main}. Because the removed cost is the only place either anchor
enters, it is the same model for both. Relative to the adopted weight $0.5$,
the error rises by a factor of $4.5$ to $7.3$ and the direction error from
$7$--$12^\circ$ to $76$--$85^\circ$ on the real sequences and from $2$ to
$28^\circ$ on the synthetic ones. Nothing else in the sweep comes close to
this effect, which is the quantitative form of Sec.~\ref{sec:beta_exp}.

Among non-zero weights the two kinds of data part ways. On the synthetic
sequences stronger is better throughout, for both anchors ($20.5$ to
$4.7^\circ$/s from $0.1$ to $0.9$ with the contrast anchor). On the real ones
the contrast anchor is best at $0.1$--$0.3$ and worse beyond (\texttt{poster}
$9.4^\circ$/s at $0.1$, $12.1$ at $0.9$), while the gyroscope anchor changes by
at most $0.3^\circ$/s anywhere from $0.3$ to $0.9$. Which anchor is used
matters far less than whether one is used at all: at every weight from $0.1$
to $0.9$ the two stay within $3.5^\circ$/s of each other, against
$43$--$54^\circ$/s for no anchor, and the gyroscope is ahead everywhere except
on \texttt{poster} at the weakest weight. This is the support we have for the
claim that what the model requires is an absolute reference, not specifically
an inertial one.

\subsubsection{Kinematic weight}
The step size $\delta_{F\!R}$ of the kinematic update tests the mechanism
proposed in Sec.~\ref{sec:speed} directly. If the magnitude shrinkage is caused
by that update out-weighting the anchor, then $\delta_{F\!R}$ should control it
monotonically. It does, over essentially the whole admissible range: the update
is a blend $\wvec \leftarrow \wvec - \delta_{F\!R}(\wvec - \wvec^\star)$, so
$\delta_{F\!R} = 1$ would replace $\wvec$ by the flow-derived estimate outright,
and across $0.02$ to $0.9$ the scale factor rises without exception on every
sequence --- $0.93 \rightarrow 1.05$ on \texttt{boxes}, $0.85 \rightarrow 1.08$
on \texttt{poster}, and $1.03 \rightarrow 1.51$ on \texttt{bicycle}. The error
follows, most sharply where the shrinkage is largest: on \texttt{bicycle} it
runs from $3.0$ to $26.5^\circ$/s and the direction error from $1.6$ to
$6.2^\circ$. A parameter the constraint system treats as arbitrary turns out to
control the dominant error. Only the ratio $\delta_{F\!R}/\delta_{\text{anchor}}$
matters (Sec.~\ref{sec:speed}), and the sweep shows it directly: the
$\delta_{\text{anchor}} = 0.1$ row and the $\delta_{F\!R} = 0.5$ row, which share
a ratio of one, agree to within $0.1^\circ$/s on every sequence.

\subsubsection{Real versus synthetic sequences}
\label{sec:realvssynth}
Along every rate axis of Table~\ref{tab:sweep}, no tested value lowers the
mean error on the real and the synthetic sequences at once. Four rates move it
substantially, and for each of them the two kinds of data are best at opposite
ends: the real sequences at $\delta_{F\!R} = 0.3$,
$\delta_{\text{anchor}} = 0.1$, $\delta_{V\!F\!G} = 0.48$ and
$\delta_{R\!F} = 0.01$, with mean errors of $8.2$, $8.5$, $8.0$ and
$8.9^\circ$/s against $9.3$ at the adopted point, and the synthetic ones at
$\delta_{F\!R} = 0.02$, $\delta_{\text{anchor}} = 0.9$,
$\delta_{V\!F\!G} = 0.03$ and $\delta_{R\!F} = 0.2$, with $3.0$, $4.8$, $2.4$
and $4.3^\circ$/s against $7.1$. Each of these values makes the other kind of
data worse, so no single configuration is best for both.

$\delta_{V\!F\!G}$ shows why. At its smallest value the network returns its
anchor: on all five sequences the error is within $0.05^\circ$/s of contrast
maximization alone (Table~\ref{tab:baseline}). Strengthening the flow
constraint lets the maps act on $\wvec$, and they move it in opposite
directions on the two kinds of data --- on \texttt{poster} from $13.8$ to
$8.8^\circ$/s at $0.48$, on \texttt{bicycle} from $2.5$ to $11.2$. They act
almost entirely on the magnitude: across the axis the direction error changes
by less than $0.1^\circ$ on the real sequences and at most $0.5^\circ$ on the
synthetic ones, while $\beta$ rises on every sequence, from $0.84$--$0.92$ to
$0.93$--$1.01$ on the real ones and from $1.01$ to $1.17$ on the synthetic
ones. Whether that shrinkage helps depends on the anchor. Contrast
maximization alone overestimates the speed on the real sequences
($\beta = 0.84$--$0.92$ in the runs of Table~\ref{tab:baseline}) and is nearly
exact on the synthetic ones ($\beta = 1.01$), so the same shrinkage corrects
the one and spoils the other. It is the shrinkage that $\delta_{F\!R}$ controls
in Sec.~\ref{sec:speed}, here set from the visual side.

All four rates set how much authority over $\wvec$ the maps hold against the
anchor: a small $\delta_{F\!R}$ or $\delta_{V\!F\!G}$, a large
$\delta_{\text{anchor}}$, or a large $\delta_{R\!F}$, which makes the flow
follow the anchored $\wvec$, all hand it to the anchor. The synthetic sequences
are best served by the anchor and the real ones by the maps because of how
good the anchor is on each (Sec.~\ref{sec:baseline}): contrast maximization
alone attains $2.4^\circ$/s on the synthetic sequences, and the maps have
almost nothing to add, but only $8.4$--$13.9^\circ$/s on the real ones, where
the network improves on it by $13$--$20\%$ at the adopted configuration and by
$15$--$37\%$ at $\delta_{V\!F\!G} = 0.48$. The synthetic columns of
Table~\ref{tab:main} should therefore be read as showing what the method does
when its anchor is already near-perfect rather than as a second independent
validation. The regularisers of Sec.~\ref{sec:regularisers} divide along the
same line: $\delta_{\text{curl}}$ helps the real sequences and harms the
synthetic ones, $\delta_{\text{shrink}I}$ the reverse.

\subsubsection{Parameters with little effect}
\label{sec:other_deltas}
$\delta_{I\!G}$ and $\delta_{G\!I}$, the two rates that couple $\Imap$ and
$\Gmap$, move the error by at most $0.5^\circ$/s on any sequence, across a
thirteen-fold and a ten-fold change. Neither is worth tuning for the angular
velocity: they govern the exchange between $\Imap$ and $\Gmap$, and $\wvec$
sees that exchange only at second hand, through the flow constraint. Their
effect on the reconstruction is larger. Raising $\delta_{G\!I}$ from $0.02$ to
$0.2$ raises the track-mean $|r|$ of the read-out on all five sequences, from
$0.27$ to $0.34$ on average, and lowers the curl share of $\Gmap$ on each real
one, from $11$ to $7\%$ on average.

Iterations per frame matter even less: across $25$ to $150$ the error changes
by at most $0.2^\circ$/s on any sequence. The $50$--$75$
of~\cite{martel2019thesis} can therefore be reduced threefold at no cost to
$\wvec$.

\subsubsection{Accumulation window}
\label{sec:window_sweep}
$\Delta t$ is the one parameter on which the two quantities this report
measures want opposite things. For the angular velocity, longer is better
across the whole range tested and on every sequence: the mean error is
$34.8^\circ$/s at $5$\,ms, $14.3$ at $10$, $9.3$ at $20$ and $7.6$ at $40$ on
the real sequences, and $33.0$, $7.1$ and $6.9$ at $10$, $20$ and $40$\,ms on
the synthetic ones, which at $5$\,ms do not track at all ($\beta$ of order
$10^4$). Short windows fail for two compounding reasons --- fewer events per
frame make the contrast estimate that anchors $\wvec$ noisier, and
$\wvec = R/\Delta t$ divides whatever error remains by a smaller number. The
degradation is not an artefact of how we score: the synthetic sequences are
scored against the simulator's exact twist, with no pose differencing
involved, and degrade the most.

For the reconstruction the ordering reverses. The track-mean $|r|$ of the
read-out falls from $0.49$ at $10$\,ms to $0.30$ at $20$ and $0.12$ at $40$ on
the real sequences ($0.51$, $0.23$ and $0.14$ on the synthetic ones), and
Sec.~\ref{sec:reconstruction} explains why. A single window cannot serve both.
This report keeps $20$\,ms, the better choice for the quantity it evaluates
numerically; Sec.~\ref{sec:image_settings} gives the configuration a system
built for the image should use instead.

\section{Experiments on Intensity Reconstruction}
\label{sec:recon_experiments}

Besides $\wvec$, the network infers $\Imap$, $\Gmap$ and $\Fmap$, which the
angular-velocity metrics do not capture. Figure~\ref{fig:maps} shows them for
the \texttt{CMax-anchor} runs of Table~\ref{tab:main} on all five sequences. On the real sequences the intensity
map is recovered well enough for the scene to be recognised although $\Imap$ is
never observed, and the gradient map concentrates on scene edges. On the two
synthetic sequences the scene is recognisable but overlaid by a diagonal
ripple, the anchored reconstruction that Sec.~\ref{sec:anchor_image}
examines. In every case the flow field varies smoothly across the image as a
pure rotation requires. Appendix~\ref{app:maps} repeats the same frames for the
other four models. On \texttt{dynamic\_rotation} the reconstruction is no
worse than on the static sequences, which is why we report it
(Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}).

The reconstruction is not, however, uniform along a track, and the figure shows
favourable moments. Every row is the last frame of its track except
\texttt{poster}, whose last frames are the tail of an excursion of the kind
described next (frames $50$--$72$); it is shown at frame $44$, the frame of highest $|r|$ on that
track ($0.75$ against a track mean of $0.19$), for \texttt{CMax-anchor} and for
the mean over all five models alike. Following $\Imap$ frame by frame on the
anchored model, its
standard deviation varies between $0.03$ and $1.59$ over the $1.5$\,s of
\texttt{poster}, with the excursions concentrated at the peaks of the rotation;
the same network without an anchor stays between $0.02$ and $0.05$ throughout.
During those excursions the map loses its structure and the gradient map reaches
the clipping bound of Sec.~\ref{sec:networks}. The angular velocity is unaffected
--- the errors of Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments} are computed over the whole track, excursions
included --- but the visual interpretation and the quantity we score are not
equally reliable at all times, and only the latter has been measured
systematically.

\subsection{Factors Affecting the Reconstruction}
\label{sec:reconstruction}

The intensity map is the one inferred quantity we have so far judged only by
eye. It is also the quantity in which our results looked least like the
published ones: where Martel and Cook show a recognisable photograph, our
$\Imap$ was closer to a relief map of the scene edges. This section and the
next two identify what that depends on: the accumulation window and the
intensity update (this section), the anchor (Sec.~\ref{sec:anchor_image}) and
the regularisation of the maps (Sec.~\ref{sec:regularisers}). The accuracy of
the angular velocity is not among them.

\subsubsection{Reconstruction metric}
We score $\Imap$ by the magnitude $|r|$ of the Pearson correlation between the
reconstruction and the APS frame nearest the end of the track, over the
interior $80\%$ of the image. The APS intensities, scaled to $[0,1]$, enter as
$\log(1+\text{APS})$, which on that range is close to linear rather than a
log-intensity like $\Imap$. Scoring against the logarithm of the 8-bit values
instead changes the final-frame $|r|$ of the runs of Table~\ref{tab:main} by
between $-0.10$ and $+0.04$ ($-0.01$ on average) and changes no ranking between
models on any sequence. The absolute correlation is used because
it is invariant to scale, offset and sign, which is exactly the freedom the
reconstruction has: neither the $\beta$-ambiguity of Sec.~\ref{sec:scale} ---
which admits $\beta < 0$ and so an inverted $\Imap$ --- nor the constant of
integration in~\eqref{eq:poisson_exact} is determined by the data, so a metric
sensitive to any of them would be measuring the gauge rather than the image.
The sign matters in practice: an unanchored network inverts $\Imap$ whenever
its $\wvec$ points away from the true one (Appendix~\ref{app:maps}), and a
signed $r$ averages such a track to zero although every frame of it is a
recognisable image. Each sequence is scored at three track lengths ($0.4$,
$0.8$, $1.2$\,s), since the reconstruction is not uniform in time and one
end-of-run snapshot is not representative (Table~\ref{tab:recon},
Fig.~\ref{fig:recon}). The $|r|$ of Tables~\ref{tab:main},
\ref{tab:regularisers} and~\ref{tab:curl} is the same quantity taken at every
frame of the $1.5$\,s track, against the APS frame nearest each, and averaged.
This is a comparison between reconstructions, not an absolute quality measure:
the APS differs in exposure and is offset in time, so the attainable $|r|$ is
well below unity even for a good map.

\subsubsection{Integrability of the gradient map}
\label{sec:curl}
One quantity explains most of what follows, so we introduce it first. Recovering
$\Imap$ from $\Gmap$ presupposes that $\Gmap$ \emph{is} the gradient of
something, and a vector field is a gradient only if it is curl-free,
$\partial_y\Gmap^{(x)} = \partial_x\Gmap^{(y)}$. Equivalently: summing $\Gmap$
around any closed path must give zero, since the path returns to the pixel it
started from and that pixel has one intensity. A field with curl describes an
impossible staircase, and no image has it as its gradient.

Nothing in the model enforces this. $\Gmap$ is pulled by two costs with
different concerns: the spatial cost blends it towards $\nabla\Imap$, which is
curl-free by construction, while the flow constraint~\eqref{eq:ofce} only
requires $\Vmap+\Fmap\!\cdot\!\Gmap=0$ at each pixel independently and couples
no neighbours at all. Their compromise is in general not curl-free, and the
aperture problem compounds it: \eqref{eq:ofce} constrains only the component of
$\Gmap$ along $\Fmap$, leaving the perpendicular component to drift. Solving
for $\Imap$ is a least-squares projection onto the gradient fields, so whatever
curl $\Gmap$ carries is discarded, and the discarded part is the error the
reconstruction inherits.

Decomposing $\Gmap$ into its curl-free and divergence-free parts measures this
directly. We quote it for one set of runs throughout, the final $\Gmap$ of the
$1.5$\,s \texttt{CMax-anchor} runs of Table~\ref{tab:main}: there
$3$--$20\%$ of the energy of $\Gmap$ is curl on the real sequences and
$18$--$29\%$ on the synthetic ones, a part of the inferred gradient map that
describes no image whatever. The share varies along a track, so these are
end-of-track values. This is not a defect of our implementation but a property of the
model, and any faithful implementation of either source will show it.

Two cautions on reading the quantity. It is \emph{not} a quality measure: at
$\Delta t=5$\,ms the curl share is under $6\%$ on \texttt{boxes} and
\texttt{poster} and under $1\%$ on the synthetic sequences, while the
angular-velocity error is the worst anywhere in this study
(Table~\ref{tab:sweep}), because at that window $\Gmap$ barely develops and a
field of nearly zero is trivially curl-free. Nor is it monotone in the window:
on \texttt{poster} it is $5$, $8$, $20$ and $6\%$ at $5$, $10$, $20$ and
$40$\,ms. What the quantity does measure is how far $\Gmap$ has been pulled
out of the gradient subspace by the flow constraint relative to its own
magnitude, and we use it below only to compare reconstructions at a fixed
window, not across windows.

\subsubsection{Accumulation window}
$\Vmap$ is formed by binning events over $\Delta t$, during which the scene
moves. At the $20$\,ms used everywhere else in this report, the image travels
$3.4$\,px per window on the real sequences and $5.0$\,px on the synthetic ones
--- wider than the structures $\Gmap$ is meant to resolve. The event frame is
therefore a smeared edge rather than a sharp one, and $\Gmap$ inherits the
smear before any intensity update sees it. The signature is visible in the
frames: the artefacts are streaks aligned with the direction of motion, running
vertically on \texttt{boxes\_rotation}, whose segment is $\omega_x$-dominant and
therefore moves the image vertically.

Halving the window to $10$\,ms halves the travel and improves the exact
read-out on all $12$ runs and the iterative update on $11$
(Table~\ref{tab:recon}). The mean $|r|$ of the exact read-out rises from $0.28$
to $0.55$, and the plain iterative update improves as well, from $0.13$ to
$0.28$ --- so this is $\Gmap$ becoming cleaner,
not an artefact of one intensity update. The effect is uneven, however, and
tracks how much the smear was actually costing: on \texttt{boxes\_rotation},
where the streaks are conspicuous, $|r|$ rises from $0.35$ to $0.48$, whereas on
\texttt{poster\_rotation}, whose texture is finer and whose artefacts are less
directional, it is unchanged at $0.42$ and $0.41$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:recon}). The
gain is also not free: at $10$\,ms each window holds half the events, so $\Vmap$
is sparser, and what is measured is the net of the two effects.

The improvement is confined to the reconstruction: a shorter window raises the
angular-velocity error (Sec.~\ref{sec:window_sweep}), so the two requirements
are opposed here rather than merely uncorrelated.

\subsubsection{Intensity update}
Given the same $\Gmap$, solving the Poisson equation
exactly~\eqref{eq:poisson_exact} instead of taking one gradient
step~\eqref{eq:cook_poisson} recovers the low frequencies the step cannot reach
in $75$ iterations. The exact (Fourier) solution beats the iterative update on
$10$ of $12$ runs at $20$\,ms and on $11$ of $12$ at $10$\,ms, and the
difference is the difference between a relief and an image: the stones of
\texttt{poster} acquire flat interiors, and in the $10$\,ms frame of
\texttt{boxes\_rotation} shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:recon} the exact read-out
reaches $|r|=0.84$, at which point the log pile, the boxes and the calibration
target are all legible.

Where the exact solve is applied matters more than which transform it uses.
Substituted for~\eqref{eq:cook_poisson} \emph{inside} the message passing it
destabilises the network: the solve amplifies each frequency of $\Gmap$ by
$\lvert k\rvert^{-2}$, so low-frequency error in $\Gmap$ integrates into large
spurious blobs, which then corrupt $\Gmap$ itself through the $\delta_{I\!G}$
coupling --- on \texttt{poster} the peak of $\lvert\Gmap\rvert$ falls from
$3.96$ to $1.40$ and the texture is lost. Applied once to the $\Gmap$ the
network has converged to, with no feedback path, the same equation behaves.
We therefore use~\eqref{eq:cook_poisson} in the loop and~\eqref{eq:poisson_exact}
as a read-out.

The transform's boundary assumption is the remaining choice, and here the
measurement contradicts the theory. The discrete cosine transform assumes
reflecting boundaries, which is what the zero-gradient border convention of our
forward differences implies, and on a synthetic test with an exact gradient
field it is exact (relative RMS $2\!\times\!10^{-13}$) where the periodic Fourier
transform is off by a wrap-around ramp (relative RMS $0.63$). On real data the
Fourier transform nevertheless scores higher on average at both windows
($0.28$ against $0.25$ at $20$\,ms, $0.55$ against $0.31$ at $10$\,ms),
winning $7$ of $12$ runs at $20$\,ms --- essentially a tie --- and $11$ of $12$
at $10$\,ms. The explanation consistent with both observations is the
measurement of Sec.~\ref{sec:curl}: the synthetic test hands both solvers a
\emph{valid} gradient field, the regime the cosine transform is exact in,
whereas the $\Gmap$ the network produces carries curl and is therefore the
gradient of no image at all. It carries low-frequency error, which the exact solve amplifies by
$\lvert k\rvert^{-2}$, and the periodic transform's wrap artefact happens to
suppress part of it. The cosine transform integrates that error faithfully,
which is correct and worse. We report the Fourier read-out.

Across the three intensity updates, on the same $1.0$\,s of \texttt{poster},
the angular-velocity error is $10.81$, $10.82$ and $10.83$\,$^\circ$/s. A
change that raises the low-frequency share of the reconstruction by a factor
of eight ($0.040$ to $0.333$) leaves the quantity we score untouched,
because the intensity map influences $\wvec$ only through $\Gmap$ and the flow
constraint, and $\wvec$ is determined by a least-squares fit over the whole flow
field that is dominated by the anchor. This is the clearest evidence for the
observation running through this section: the two things the model is asked to
produce --- an angular velocity and a picture --- are not improved by the same
choices, and a configuration tuned for one should not be assumed good for the
other.

\subsection{Anchoring and Image Quality}
\label{sec:anchor_image}
The external anchor, which is what makes the angular velocity usable at all,
degrades $\Imap$: the excursions of $\Imap$ described at the start of
Sec.~\ref{sec:recon_experiments} occur only with the anchor. The anchor makes
the system
inconsistent --- it forces $\wvec$, hence $\Fmap$, to a value the flow
constraint would not have chosen --- and $\Imap$ absorbs the disagreement.

Across models the picture and the angular velocity are not merely
uncorrelated but opposed. Figure~\ref{fig:dissociation} shows the same
final frame of \texttt{boxes}, \texttt{bicycle} and \texttt{street}
reconstructed by the unanchored network and by the two anchored ones. Without
an anchor $|r|$ is $0.63$, $0.60$ and $0.56$ --- the synthetic scenes legible
down to the shop signage --- against $0.41$--$0.45$, $0.13$ and $0.32$--$0.33$
for the anchored models, whose synthetic reconstructions are ripple; the
angular-velocity error is $49$--$52^\circ$/s against $5.8$--$7.3$.
\texttt{poster} follows the same pattern ($0.53$ against $0.19$--$0.26$ in
Table~\ref{tab:main}), and \texttt{dynamic} is the one sequence on which the
unanchored and anchored pictures are level ($0.36$ against $0.29$--$0.35$).

The unanchored network gets the picture wrong only in a way the data cannot
detect: its $\Imap$ inverts whenever its $\wvec$ points away from the true one
(Appendix~\ref{app:maps}), which is the sign freedom $|r|$ is built to ignore.
This is also the answer to an expectation worth recording: noise-free rendered
data does reconstruct well, but only when the network is left to its own
devices.

\subsection{Regularisation and Image-Optimised Configuration}

\subsubsection{Regularisation of the maps}
\label{sec:regularisers}

Neither source regularises $\Gmap$, and the inferred maps show the consequence:
$\lvert\Gmap\rvert$ reaches the clipping bound of
Sec.~\ref{sec:networks}, and $\Imap$ makes the excursions described at the
start of Sec.~\ref{sec:recon_experiments}. The flow
constraint~\eqref{eq:ofce} is the likely culprit, since it is the one relation
that touches each pixel independently: it can grow a few pixels without any
neighbour objecting. Guo and Gallego report the same failure in their
event-based mosaicing bundle adjustment~\cite{guo2024emba}, where each residual
likewise depends on the map gradient at a single pixel, and add a shrinkage
prior to suppress it.

We test three regularising terms. They are of different provenance, which
matters for how much weight to give each result, so we state it plainly.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{$\delta_{\text{shrink}I}$, on $\Imap$.} The leading $\Imap$ of
  the thesis' Eq.~6.61, which Sec.~\ref{sec:deviations} records us as having
  omitted. Setting it to $1$ restores the published update exactly; Cook's
  Eq.~9 has no counterpart, being a convex blend whose two $\Imap$ terms
  cancel.
\item \textbf{$\delta_{\text{map}}$, on $\Gmap$.} The prior
  $\eta\lVert\Gmap\rVert^2$ of~\cite{guo2024emba}, giving
  $\Gmap \leftarrow (1-2\delta_{\text{map}})\Gmap$. Not in either source
  implemented here.
\item \textbf{$\delta_{\text{curl}}$, on $\Gmap$.} Ours. Sec.~\ref{sec:curl}
  showed that part of $\Gmap$ is curl and therefore the gradient of no image
  at all, and that the intensity solve discards exactly that part. This
  term penalises $\lVert\Gmap - P\Gmap\rVert^2$, with $P$ the projection onto
  the curl-free fields, so a fraction $\delta_{\text{curl}}$ of the curl is
  removed each iteration.
\end{enumerate}

Table~\ref{tab:regularisers} reports all three. No term is best everywhere:
$\delta_{\text{curl}} = 0.6$ leads the angular-velocity and read-out columns
but is worse on both synthetic sequences, and $\delta_{\text{map}} = 0.03$
leads the in-loop and blow-up columns but not the read-out.

The thesis' own term is the weakest of the three. Restoring it leaves the
angular velocity unchanged on all three real sequences and the reconstruction
within noise of the unregularised network; its one clear effect is on the
synthetic pair, where the error falls from $7.1$ to $6.7^\circ$/s. It also
\emph{lowers} the quality of the in-loop intensity map, from $0.149$ to $0.114$.
This is worth stating because it closes off an obvious hypothesis: the omission
recorded in Sec.~\ref{sec:deviations} is not what separated our reconstructions
from the published ones. The choice of Poisson solver
(Sec.~\ref{sec:reconstruction}) was.

The prior of~\cite{guo2024emba} does what it is advertised to do, and only
that. It removes the excursions of $\Imap$ completely --- the fraction of the
track spent blown up falls from $0.063$ to $0.000$, and on
\texttt{poster\_rotation} from $0.17$ to $0.00$ --- and gives the best in-loop
map of any configuration tested ($0.212$). It buys no improvement in the
read-out reconstruction ($0.257 \rightarrow 0.252$) and costs a little on
\texttt{dynamic}. Read as a
stability device rather than a quality one, which is how its authors present
it, it succeeds.

The curl term is the only one that improves the reconstruction, and it does so
on every sequence: $|r|$ against the APS rises from $0.257$ to
$0.353$ on average and improves on all five individually. It also gives the
best angular velocity on all three real sequences. Against that, it is
\emph{worse} on both synthetic ones ($7.1 \rightarrow 7.7$ and
$7.9^\circ$/s), so the
real-synthetic split of Sec.~\ref{sec:realvssynth} reappears here with the
opposite sign to $\delta_{\text{shrink}I}$. Two cautions attach to it. It is
our own construction, not something either source or the mosaicing work
proposes, and we have no independent validation of it. And its effect on the
curl share inside the closed loop is not what the term does in isolation:
applied to a fixed field it provably drives the curl to zero, but in the
running network the share of the final $\Gmap$ falls below $1\%$ only on
\texttt{boxes} and \texttt{dynamic}, and \emph{rises} on \texttt{poster}
($0.20 \rightarrow 0.96$) and on the synthetic sequences
($0.29 \rightarrow 0.56$ and $0.18 \rightarrow 0.35$), which we cannot
presently explain.

Figure~\ref{fig:regularisers} shows what the three do to the image on four
sequences. The differences are small on \texttt{boxes}, where the
unregularised reconstruction is already good, and largest on
\texttt{poster\_rotation}, where only the curl term recovers the stones at all.

None of the three is adopted in the configuration of Table~\ref{tab:params};
all results elsewhere in this report are without them. We report
$\delta_{\text{curl}}$ because it is the only term that improves the
reconstruction on every sequence tested, and we do not adopt it because it is
our own addition rather than a published one, and because its benefit does not
extend to the synthetic sequences. Table~\ref{tab:curl} gives the full
comparison against Table~\ref{tab:main}, model by model.

\subsubsection{Image-optimised configuration}
\label{sec:image_settings}
Every number above was measured at the configuration of
Table~\ref{tab:params}, which was chosen for $\wvec$ alone
(Sec.~\ref{sec:objective}), and that configuration remains the one behind every
angular-velocity result in this report. The three levers this section has
identified for the picture --- the accumulation window, the integrability term
and the anchor --- can instead be set for the image. We search them jointly:
$\Delta t \in \{20, 10\}$\,ms, $\delta_{\text{curl}} \in \{0, 0.6\}$, and
\texttt{thesis} without an anchor against \texttt{CMax-anchor}, every other
parameter at Table~\ref{tab:params}, $1.5$\,s per run, scored by the
track-mean $|r|$ of the read-out~\eqref{eq:poisson_exact}
(Table~\ref{tab:image}). The $20$\,ms points are the runs of
Tables~\ref{tab:main} and~\ref{tab:curl}. Since Sec.~\ref{sec:realvssynth}
finds that real and synthetic sequences want opposite settings for $\wvec$, the
best configuration is chosen separately for each kind of data.

They want opposite settings for the image as well. On the real sequences the
best picture keeps the anchor: \texttt{CMax-anchor} at $10$\,ms with
$\delta_{\text{curl}} = 0.6$ raises the mean $|r|$ from $0.30$ to $0.55$ while
its angular-velocity error rises only from $9.3$ to $14.0^\circ$/s, so the
image-optimised configuration still tracks. On the synthetic sequences the best
picture drops the anchor: the unanchored network at $20$\,ms with

network on all five sequences and degrades the unanchored one on all five, and
on synthetic data it raises the anchored error from $7.1$ to $33.0^\circ$/s,
the cost of short windows found in Sec.~\ref{sec:window_sweep}.

the stones of \texttt{poster}, barely visible at the configuration for
$\wvec$, and the \texttt{bicycle} and \texttt{street} scenes, ripple there,
become legible.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have re-implemented both published formulations of Interacting Maps --- the
sequential relaxation of Cook \emph{et al.} and the energy-based message passing
of Martel's dissertation --- on a shared camera model, data path and evaluation
harness, and evaluated them on five sequences against an independent reference.
To our knowledge no implementation of either was previously available.

The clearest result is negative and concerns the method as published. Neither
formulation produces a usable angular velocity: direction errors of
$76$--$85^\circ$ on the real sequences mean the recovered axis is
uninformative, and the magnitude can leave any plausible range entirely,
reaching $\beta = 338$ on \texttt{street} while the maps remain mutually
consistent (Table~\ref{tab:main}). This is
not a tuning failure but the scale ambiguity of~\eqref{eq:beta} resolving itself
arbitrarily, and warm-starting from the correct value does not prevent it: the
estimate leaves the initial scale as soon as the relaxation is free to rescale
$\Gmap$ against $\Fmap$. On the real sequences the choice of update scheme is,
by comparison, immaterial: Gauss--Seidel and Jacobi differ by $5$--$10\%$ and
fail in the same way. On the synthetic ones the Gauss--Seidel network is the
less stable of the two (Sec.~\ref{sec:converge}).

What makes the method work is an absolute reference inside the cost that is
minimised at every iteration. Supplied by a gyroscope, as the thesis proposes,
it reduces the error by six to eight times and brings the direction error to
$1$--$10^\circ$. Our main positive result is that this reference need not be a
measurement: a contrast-maximization estimate computed from the same events
occupies the same place in the model and comes within $1.5$--$2.6^\circ$/s of
the inertial anchor on the three real sequences, without a second sensor.
On the fastest real segment the gap reaches $4.7^\circ$/s. We do not claim parity --- the gyroscope remains ahead
throughout --- but the gap is small next to what the anchor buys. On the real
sequences the gyro-anchored network also matches its own gyroscope against the
motion-capture reference to within a few tenths of a degree per second, so the
visual constraints are not simply overridden by the inertial signal.

We also characterised the residual error. It is almost entirely a magnitude
error, it grows with rotation speed ($r = +0.96$ over seven segments at the
original step size, $+0.60$ at the adopted one), and it is
produced by the kinematic cost fitting $\wvec$ to a flow field that the visual
side systematically underestimates: it appears only when that cost updates
$\wvec$, and the ratio of its step size to the anchor weight controls it
monotonically across the whole admissible range. Two candidate explanations
were tested and rejected (Sec.~\ref{sec:speed}): value clipping, and scene
content, which two synthetic sequences sharing a trajectory but differing in
scene leave unchanged. Why both synthetic sequences sit well above the trend
fitted to the real ones remains open.

Separating the two quantities the model produces turned out to matter more than
tuning either. A good angular velocity and a good picture are not obtained by
the same choices, and on the accumulation window they are in direct opposition:
from $10$ to $40$\,ms the angular-velocity error falls on every sequence while
the track-mean $|r|$ of the reconstruction falls from $0.49$ to $0.12$ on the
real ones (Sec.~\ref{sec:window_sweep}).
The intensity update makes the point most sharply --- solving the Poisson
equation exactly instead of by one gradient step changes the low-frequency
content of the reconstruction by a factor of eight and $\wvec$ by
$0.02^\circ$/s. Any configuration reported for this model should therefore say
which of the two it was chosen for.

Four practical findings are worth recording. Lens distortion should be carried
in the kinematic matrix rather than removed from the events, not for geometric
reasons --- the two routes agree to $0.043$\,px --- but because rectifying event
coordinates and re-binning them destroys about a fifth of the spatial support of
$\Vmap$ at exactly the scale the gradient operates on. Twenty-five iterations
per frame suffice where the thesis uses $50$--$75$. The intensity map should be
read out with the exact frequency-domain solution rather than taken from the
iterative update, whose convergence rate per spatial frequency leaves the low
frequencies unformed. And the tighter of the two contrast-maximization
couplings, in which a gradient step on the contrast replaces the update rule for
$\wvec$, is not robust: its step size scales with the square of the event count
and it diverged outright on the densest sequence.

\subsection*{Outlook}

The most immediate opportunity follows from the sensitivity analysis. The
parameter that matters most, the ratio of $\delta_{F\!R}$ to
$\delta_{\text{anchor}}$, was found by grid search, yet the network already computes the quantities that indicate
how they should be set --- the per-relation residuals. Adapting them online from
those residuals, rather than fixing them in advance, would remove the one part
of this system that currently requires a human.

A more substantial extension is to stop treating contrast maximization as an
external anchor. At present the events are used twice: once through $\Vmap$ and
once, independently, inside the estimator. Optimising the contrast of the warped
events \emph{jointly} with the map relaxation --- rather than solving one and
injecting the answer into the other --- would make motion compensation part of
the network instead of a component bolted onto it, and is the natural way to
express contrast maximization in the quantity-centric framework of
Sec.~\ref{sec:martel}. The failure of the tighter coupling reported here
suggests this is worth doing carefully rather than by simply interleaving the
two optimisations.

Finally, the model itself invites extension in the direction both sources
indicate: adding a persistent scene map so that information survives beyond one
frame, and relaxing the pure-rotation assumption, which would require depth to
enter the network.
